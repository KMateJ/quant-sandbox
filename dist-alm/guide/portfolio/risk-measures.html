Value at Risk and expected shortfall

Lesson 8 of 10 · 1 min read

The lesson "Returns and volatility" measured risk by the volatility σ\sigmaσ, the standard deviation of returns. A risk manager usually wants a different kind of answer: an amount of money that the position could lose. This lesson turns a return distribution into two such numbers, Value at Risk and expected shortfall, first under the normal model and then with fat tails, and ends with portfolios, which links back to "Diversification and correlation".

The loss distribution

Take a position worth VVV today and fix a horizon, for example one trading day or ten. Let RRR be the simple return of the position over that horizon. The loss is the fall in value, counted as a positive number:

L=−(Vh−V)=−VR.L = -(V_h - V) = -V R.L=−(Vh​−V)=−VR.

A gain is a negative loss. The distribution of LLL is the loss distribution; risk measures are numbers read off its right tail, where the large losses are. We assume the position is held unchanged over the horizon.

Value at Risk

Pick a confidence level α\alphaα, typically 95% or 99%. The Value at Risk VaRα\text{VaR}_\alphaVaRα​ is the α\alphaα-quantile of the loss distribution: the smallest amount ℓ\ellℓ such that the loss stays at or below ℓ\ellℓ with probability at least α\alphaα,

VaRα=min⁡{ℓ:P(L≤ℓ)≥α}.\text{VaR}_\alpha = \min\{\ell : \mathbb{P}(L \le \ell) \ge \alpha\}.VaRα​=min{ℓ:P(L≤ℓ)≥α}.

A one-day 99% VaR of 28,000 reads: on 99 days out of 100 the loss should not exceed 28,000. The convention here is that VaR is a positive amount of money and α\alphaα is close to 1.

Expected shortfall

VaR is a threshold. It says how often the threshold is crossed, but nothing about how far. The expected shortfall answers that: it is the average loss in the worst 1−α1-\alpha1−α fraction of outcomes. For a continuous loss distribution this equals

ESα=E[L∣L≥VaRα]=11−α∫α1VaRu du.\text{ES}_\alpha = \mathbb{E}\big[L \mid L \ge \text{VaR}_\alpha\big] = \frac{1}{1-\alpha}\int_\alpha^1 \text{VaR}_u\,du.ESα​=E[L∣L≥VaRα​]=1−α1​∫α1​VaRu​du.

The second form shows it as the average of all VaRs beyond α\alphaα, so ESα≥VaRα\text{ES}_\alpha \ge \text{VaR}_\alphaESα​≥VaRα​ always.

Scaling with the horizon

Volatility is usually estimated from daily returns. As shown in "Returns and volatility", if daily returns are independent and identically distributed, variances add over days, so over hhh days

σh=σ1h,μh=μ1h.\sigma_h = \sigma_1\sqrt{h}, \qquad \mu_h = \mu_1 h.σh​=σ1​h​,μh​=μ1​h.

Over short horizons μ\muμ is tiny compared with σ\sigmaσ and is often set to zero; then VaR and ES grow like h\sqrt{h}h​. The rule relies on three assumptions: returns on different days are independent, volatility stays constant, and the position is not changed during the horizon. Volatility clustering and autocorrelation break the first two, so a scaled 10-day figure is an approximation.

A position of V=1,000,000V = 1{,}000{,}000V=1,000,000 has daily volatility σ=1.2%\sigma = 1.2\%σ=1.2% and μ=0\mu = 0μ=0. The steps below compute its 99% VaR and ES over one day and its VaR over ten days.

Fat tails

Daily returns have more extreme days than the normal predicts. A simple fat-tailed model is the Student t distribution with ν\nuν degrees of freedom. Its variance is ν/(ν−2)\nu/(\nu-2)ν/(ν−2), so we rescale it by (ν−2)/ν\sqrt{(\nu-2)/\nu}(ν−2)/ν​ to unit variance and multiply by σ\sigmaσ. Both models then have the same volatility and differ only in shape: the t puts more weight in the far tail and in the centre, less in between. Small ν\nuν means fat tails; as ν→∞\nu \to \inftyν→∞ the t becomes normal.

For ν=4\nu = 4ν=4 the multipliers of σ\sigmaσ are:

  • α=97.5%\alpha = 97.5\%α=97.5%: VaR 1.96 under both models; ES 2.34 (normal) against 2.82 (t).
  • α=99%\alpha = 99\%α=99%: VaR 2.33 against 2.65 (14% higher); ES 2.67 against 3.69 (38% higher).
  • α=99.9%\alpha = 99.9\%α=99.9%: VaR 3.09 against 5.07; ES 3.37 against 6.84, about twice as much.

In the worked example the fat-tailed one-day 99% VaR is about 31,794 and the ES about 44,280. A VaR at a moderate confidence level hardly sees the tail; the ES, which averages over the tail, does, and the gap widens as α\alphaα rises.

Historical simulation

Instead of assuming a distribution, historical simulation reuses the past. Take, say, the last 500 daily returns, apply each to today's position to get 500 hypothetical losses, and sort them. The 99% VaR is about the fifth-largest loss and the ES the average of the five largest. The method captures fat tails and correlations as they actually occurred, but only those in the window: a calm window gives a low VaR right before a crisis.

Properties of VaR and ES

Because VaR is a single quantile, two positions with the same VaR can have very different losses beyond it. Worse, VaR is not subadditive: the VaR of a portfolio can exceed the sum of the VaRs of its parts. Example: two independent bonds each lose 100 with probability 4% and nothing otherwise. Each has a 95% VaR of 0, since a loss occurs with probability below 5%. Holding both, the probability of at least one default is 1−0.962=7.84%1 - 0.96^2 = 7.84\%1−0.962=7.84%, so the portfolio's 95% VaR is 100. Measured by VaR, diversification looks harmful.

Expected shortfall is subadditive, ES(L1+L2)≤ES(L1)+ES(L2)\text{ES}(L_1 + L_2) \le \text{ES}(L_1) + \text{ES}(L_2)ES(L1​+L2​)≤ES(L1​)+ES(L2​); in the example each bond has ES 80 and the pair 103.2, well below 160. Together with three simpler properties (monotonicity, translation invariance, positive homogeneity) this makes ES a coherent risk measure.

VaR answers "how bad can it get on a normal bad day"; ES answers "how bad is it on average once that line is crossed". For this reason the Basel Fundamental Review of the Trading Book (FRTB) replaced 99% VaR by 97.5% ES for market risk capital. Under the normal model the two are almost equal (2.326 against 2.338 standard deviations); with fat tails ES is larger.

Portfolio VaR

For a portfolio with weights www and return covariance matrix Σ\SigmaΣ, the lesson "Diversification and correlation" gives σp2=w⊤Σ w\sigma_p^2 = w^\top\Sigma\,wσp2​=w⊤Σw, and in the normal model VaRα=V(σpzα−μp)\text{VaR}_\alpha = V(\sigma_p z_\alpha - \mu_p)VaRα​=V(σp​zα​−μp​). With correlations below one, σp\sigma_pσp​ is less than the weighted average of the volatilities, so the portfolio VaR is less than the sum of the separate VaRs. Splitting the 1,000,000 above into two positions of 500,000, each with daily volatility 1.2% and correlation 0.3, gives σp=1.2%×0.65≈0.97%\sigma_p = 1.2\% \times \sqrt{0.65} \approx 0.97\%σp​=1.2%×0.65​≈0.97% and a 99% VaR of about 22,507 instead of 27,916.

Summary

  • The loss over a horizon is L=−VRL = -VRL=−VR; VaRα\text{VaR}_\alphaVaRα​ is its α\alphaα-quantile, a positive amount of money.
  • ESα\text{ES}_\alphaESα​ is the average loss in the worst 1−α1-\alpha1−α tail and is never below VaR.
  • Normal model: VaRα=V(σzα−μ)\text{VaR}_\alpha = V(\sigma z_\alpha - \mu)VaRα​=V(σzα​−μ), ESα=V(σφ(zα)/(1−α)−μ)\text{ES}_\alpha = V(\sigma\varphi(z_\alpha)/(1-\alpha) - \mu)ESα​=V(σφ(zα​)/(1−α)−μ), with σh=σ1h\sigma_h = \sigma_1\sqrt{h}σh​=σ1​h​ under independence and constant volatility.
  • With fat tails VaR rises moderately but ES much more, increasingly so at high confidence levels.
  • ES is subadditive and coherent, VaR is not; regulation (FRTB) uses 97.5% ES.
