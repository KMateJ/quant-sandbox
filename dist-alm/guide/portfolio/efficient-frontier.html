The efficient frontier and the capital market line

Lesson 3 of 10 · 1 min read

The lesson "Diversification and correlation" showed how the expected return and volatility of a portfolio follow from the weights, volatilities and correlations of its assets. This lesson asks which of those portfolios are worth holding. It first sorts the risky portfolios into good and bad ones, then adds a risk-free asset, which turns the choice into a single line: the capital market line.

Mean–variance preferences

Throughout the lesson we assume that investors judge a portfolio only by its expected return μ\muμ and its volatility σ\sigmaσ over one period. They prefer a higher μ\muμ when σ\sigmaσ is the same, and a lower σ\sigmaσ when μ\muμ is the same. A portfolio dominates another if it is at least as good in both respects and strictly better in one. Investors may differ in how much extra return they demand for extra risk, but no one holds a dominated portfolio.

Short sales

A short sale means borrowing an asset, selling it, and buying it back later to return it. It gives a negative weight. With wA=−0.5w_A = -0.5wA​=−0.5 and wB=1.5w_B = 1.5wB​=1.5 the investor sells A worth half of the own capital and invests one and a half times the capital in B. The formulas of the previous lesson still apply, since they never required the weights to be positive:

μp=−0.5×6%+1.5×12%=15%,σp2=0.25×0.0225+2.25×0.09−2×0.75×0.009=0.194625.\mu_p = -0.5 \times 6\% + 1.5 \times 12\% = 15\%, \qquad \sigma_p^2 = 0.25 \times 0.0225 + 2.25 \times 0.09 - 2 \times 0.75 \times 0.009 = 0.194625.μp​=−0.5×6%+1.5×12%=15%,σp2​=0.25×0.0225+2.25×0.09−2×0.75×0.009=0.194625.

This is for the assets of the previous lesson (μA=6%\mu_A = 6\%μA​=6%, σA=15%\sigma_A = 15\%σA​=15%, μB=12%\mu_B = 12\%μB​=12%, σB=30%\sigma_B = 30\%σB​=30%, ρ=0.2\rho = 0.2ρ=0.2, so σAB=0.009\sigma_{AB} = 0.009σAB​=0.009), and gives σp≈44.1%\sigma_p \approx 44.1\%σp​≈44.1%. Short selling extends the curve of attainable portfolios beyond the two assets in both directions.

The attainable set and the efficient frontier

Letting the weight w=wBw = w_Bw=wB​ run over all real numbers traces a curve in the (σ,μ)(\sigma, \mu)(σ,μ) plane: the set of attainable portfolios for two assets. With more assets it becomes a region whose left edge has the same shape. The leftmost point is the minimum-variance portfolio from the previous lesson, here wB∗≈0.143w_B^{*} \approx 0.143wB∗​≈0.143 with σ≈14.34%\sigma \approx 14.34\%σ≈14.34% and μ≈6.86%\mu \approx 6.86\%μ≈6.86%.

The minimum-variance portfolio splits the curve into two branches. Every point on the lower branch is dominated by the point on the upper branch with the same volatility. Example: at σ=20%\sigma = 20\%σ=20% the lower branch has wB≈−0.31w_B \approx -0.31wB​≈−0.31 and μ≈4.14%\mu \approx 4.14\%μ≈4.14%, while the upper branch has wB≈0.60w_B \approx 0.60wB​≈0.60 and μ≈9.58%\mu \approx 9.58\%μ≈9.58%. Nobody would choose the first.

The efficient frontier is the upper branch of the attainable set, from the minimum-variance portfolio upwards. Every portfolio on it gives the highest expected return available for its volatility.

Adding a risk-free asset

Now add a risk-free asset with return rfr_frf​, for example a short government bill held to maturity. Its volatility is zero and its covariance with every risky asset is zero. Put a fraction yyy of the money in a risky portfolio P and 1−y1 - y1−y in the risk-free asset. By the formulas of the previous lesson

μ=(1−y) rf+y μP=rf+y (μP−rf),σ=y σP(y≥0).\mu = (1-y)\,r_f + y\,\mu_P = r_f + y\,(\mu_P - r_f), \qquad \sigma = y\,\sigma_P \quad (y \ge 0).μ=(1−y)rf​+yμP​=rf​+y(μP​−rf​),σ=yσP​(y≥0).

The variance has no cross term because the covariance is zero. Eliminating y=σ/σPy = \sigma/\sigma_Py=σ/σP​ gives a straight line from the point (0,rf)(0, r_f)(0,rf​) through P:

μ=rf+μP−rfσP σ.\mu = r_f + \frac{\mu_P - r_f}{\sigma_P}\,\sigma.μ=rf​+σP​μP​−rf​​σ.

Points between (0,rf)(0, r_f)(0,rf​) and P mean lending at rfr_frf​ (0≤y≤10 \le y \le 10≤y≤1). Points beyond P mean borrowing at rfr_frf​ to invest more than one's own capital in P (y>1y > 1y>1); here we assume one can borrow at the same rate.

The Sharpe ratio and the tangency portfolio

The slope of that line is the Sharpe ratio of P,

SRP=μP−rfσP,\text{SR}_P = \frac{\mu_P - r_f}{\sigma_P},SRP​=σP​μP​−rf​​,

the excess return over rfr_frf​ per unit of volatility. A steeper line gives more expected return at every level of volatility, so the investor wants the risky portfolio with the largest Sharpe ratio. Rotating the line upward around (0,rf)(0, r_f)(0,rf​), the last attainable portfolio it touches is where it is tangent to the efficient frontier. This is the tangency portfolio T.

Write ei=μi−rfe_i = \mu_i - r_fei​=μi​−rf​ for the excess returns. Maximising SR\text{SR}SR over the weights (setting the derivative with respect to wBw_BwB​ to zero) gives weights proportional to

wA∝σB2 eA−σAB eB,wB∝σA2 eB−σAB eA,w_A \propto \sigma_B^2\, e_A - \sigma_{AB}\, e_B, \qquad w_B \propto \sigma_A^2\, e_B - \sigma_{AB}\, e_A,wA​∝σB2​eA​−σAB​eB​,wB​∝σA2​eB​−σAB​eA​,

scaled so that they add up to one. With NNN assets the same result reads w∝Σ−1(μ−rf1)w \propto \Sigma^{-1}(\mu - r_f\mathbf{1})w∝Σ−1(μ−rf​1), where Σ\SigmaΣ is the covariance matrix.

The capital market line

The line through the tangency portfolio is the capital market line (CML):

μ=rf+μT−rfσT σ.\mu = r_f + \frac{\mu_T - r_f}{\sigma_T}\,\sigma.μ=rf​+σT​μT​−rf​​σ.

Apart from the single point T, it lies above the efficient frontier. Once a risk-free asset is available, every risky-only portfolio other than T is dominated by some point on the CML. At σ=20%\sigma = 20\%σ=20%, for example, the CML gives 2%+0.391×20%≈9.82%2\% + 0.391 \times 20\% \approx 9.82\%2%+0.391×20%≈9.82%, more than the 9.58%9.58\%9.58% of the frontier portfolio with the same volatility.

Two-fund separation

Every efficient choice is therefore a mix of two funds: the risk-free asset and the tangency portfolio. Investors differ only in the proportion yyy, not in the composition of their risky holdings. This result is called two-fund separation. Think of a kitchen that serves one soup in different portion sizes, topped up with water to taste: the recipe is the same for everyone.

At the numbers above, a cautious investor who accepts σ=10%\sigma = 10\%σ=10% holds y=10/16.55≈0.604y = 10/16.55 \approx 0.604y=10/16.55≈0.604 in T and 0.3960.3960.396 in the risk-free asset, for μ≈5.91%\mu \approx 5.91\%μ≈5.91%. Without the risk-free asset this volatility would not be reachable at all, since the minimum is 14.34%. A bolder investor who accepts σ=20%\sigma = 20\%σ=20% holds y≈1.208y \approx 1.208y≈1.208, borrowing about 20.8% of the capital at rfr_frf​, for μ≈9.82%\mu \approx 9.82\%μ≈9.82%. Both hold A and B in the same ratio of 58.8 to 41.2.

Summary

  • With short sales allowed, the attainable portfolios form a curve whose upper branch, from the minimum-variance portfolio up, is the efficient frontier.
  • Mixing a risky portfolio P with a risk-free asset gives a line from (0,rf)(0, r_f)(0,rf​) through P with slope equal to the Sharpe ratio (μP−rf)/σP(\mu_P - r_f)/\sigma_P(μP​−rf​)/σP​.
  • The tangency portfolio maximises the Sharpe ratio; in the example wB≈0.412w_B \approx 0.412wB​≈0.412, μT≈8.47%\mu_T \approx 8.47\%μT​≈8.47%, σT≈16.55%\sigma_T \approx 16.55\%σT​≈16.55%, SR≈0.391\text{SR} \approx 0.391SR≈0.391.
  • The capital market line μ=rf+SRT σ\mu = r_f + \text{SR}_T\,\sigmaμ=rf​+SRT​σ dominates every other risky portfolio.
  • Two-fund separation: every investor holds the risk-free asset and the tangency portfolio, only in different proportions.
