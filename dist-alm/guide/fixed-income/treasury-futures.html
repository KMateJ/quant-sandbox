Bond futures and the cheapest-to-deliver

Lesson 6 of 10 · 1 min read

A bond future is a forward contract on a government bond, traded on an exchange. Its twist is that the seller may choose which bond to deliver from a list. This lesson explains how the exchange makes different bonds comparable with conversion factors, what the seller actually pays and receives at delivery, and why the cheapest bond to deliver changes when yields move. It builds on "Bond prices and yields", "Duration and convexity" and "Arbitrage and the forward price".

Bond futures

A bond future obliges the seller (the short) to deliver a government bond to the buyer (the long) at a fixed date, the delivery date, for a price agreed today. As with any forward, nothing changes hands when the contract is opened; the quoted futures price FFF is the price at which delivery will take place. Throughout this track bonds have face value 100, so FFF here always means the futures price, quoted per 100 of face.

Exchanges standardise everything except one detail. Instead of naming a single bond, the contract lists a deliverable basket: every government bond within a range of remaining maturities, say 8.5 to 10.5 years for a ten-year contract. The seller decides which bond in the basket to deliver. This choice is the delivery option, and it belongs to the seller.

The notional bond and conversion factors

The bonds in the basket have different coupons and maturities, so they have very different prices. A 7% bond is worth far more than a 3% bond of similar maturity. Paying the same amount for either would make the choice trivial. The exchange therefore defines a notional bond with a fixed coupon, 6% for many contracts, and quotes the futures price as if that bond were delivered.

Invoice price and delivery cost

When the seller delivers a bond, the buyer pays the invoice price per 100 of face:

invoice=F⋅CF+AI,\text{invoice} = F \cdot CF + AI,invoice=F⋅CF+AI,

where AIAIAI is the accrued interest the bond has built up since its last coupon. The conversion factor scales the futures price up for valuable bonds and down for cheap ones.

To deliver, the seller must own the bond, which costs its market (clean) price PPP plus the same accrued interest. Accrued interest appears on both sides and cancels, so the seller's net delivery cost, also called the net basis, is

net cost=P−F⋅CF.\text{net cost} = P - F \cdot CF.net cost=P−F⋅CF.

The seller delivers the bond with the smallest net cost: the cheapest-to-deliver bond, or CTD. Equivalently, the CTD is the bond with the lowest ratio P/CFP/CFP/CF.

The futures price at delivery

At the delivery date the futures price cannot exceed P/CFP/CFP/CF for any bond in the basket. If it did, a trader could buy that bond, sell the future, deliver at once and keep F⋅CF−P>0F \cdot CF - P > 0F⋅CF−P>0, an arbitrage of the kind in "Arbitrage and the forward price". Competition among sellers pushes FFF down to the lowest such ratio:

F=min⁡iPiCFi.F = \min_i \frac{P_i}{CF_i}.F=imin​CFi​Pi​​.

The CTD then has a net cost of exactly zero and every other bond a positive one. Before delivery the futures price is set by the CTD in the same way, plus the carry from "Arbitrage and the forward price"; this lesson stays at the delivery date.

At delivery F=min⁡iPi/CFiF = \min_i P_i/CF_iF=mini​Pi​/CFi​. The bond that attains the minimum is the cheapest-to-deliver, and its net cost P−F⋅CFP - F\cdot CFP−F⋅CF is zero.

The cheapest bond and the yield level

If all bonds yielded exactly 6%, every price would equal 100⋅CF100 \cdot CF100⋅CF, every ratio P/CFP/CFP/CF would be 100, and all bonds would be equally cheap. The conversion factors are exact only at the notional yield.

At any other yield the ratio P/CFP/CFP/CF is the bond's price relative to its price at 6%. From "Duration and convexity", that relative change depends on duration. At a 6% yield the Macaulay durations of A, B and C are about 8.59, 10.66 and 13.16 years.

  • When yields rise above 6%, all prices fall, and the long-duration bond C falls the most in relative terms. It has the lowest P/CFP/CFP/CF and becomes the CTD.
  • When yields fall below 6%, all prices rise, and the short-duration bond A rises the least. It now has the lowest P/CFP/CFP/CF and becomes the CTD.

The value of the delivery option

Because the seller always picks the cheapest bond, the futures price is lower than it would be for any single named bond. The buyer accepts this in exchange for a liquid, standardised contract. The further yields move from 6%, the more the bonds' costs spread out, and the more the choice is worth to the seller. Traders who hedge with bond futures must therefore track which bond is the CTD: the future's price sensitivity follows the duration of the CTD, and it jumps when the CTD switches.

Summary

  • A bond future lets the seller deliver any bond from a basket; this is the delivery option.
  • The conversion factor of a bond is its price per 1 of face at the notional 6% yield. The invoice price is F⋅CF+AIF\cdot CF + AIF⋅CF+AI.
  • The net delivery cost is P−F⋅CFP - F\cdot CFP−F⋅CF. At delivery F=min⁡iPi/CFiF = \min_i P_i/CF_iF=mini​Pi​/CFi​, and the bond attaining it is the cheapest-to-deliver.
  • Below the notional yield the lowest-duration bond tends to be the CTD, above it the highest-duration bond.
