Bond prices and yields

Lesson 1 of 10 · 1 min read

A bond is the simplest traded security whose value comes entirely from discounting: it promises fixed payments on fixed dates, and its price is what those payments are worth today. This lesson prices a bond, defines its yield and explains why prices and yields move in opposite directions. It builds on "Interest, compounding and discounting".

A bond as a list of cash flows

A plain fixed-rate bond has a face value FFF (also called par or principal), an annual coupon rate ccc and a maturity of TTT years. Throughout this track we take F=100F = 100F=100 and assume one coupon per year. The holder receives a coupon cFcFcF at the end of each year t=1,…,Tt = 1, \dots, Tt=1,…,T and, together with the last coupon, the face value FFF.

Example: a 10-year bond with c=5%c = 5\%c=5% pays 5 at the end of years 1 to 9 and 5+100=1055 + 100 = 1055+100=105 at the end of year 10. Nothing else is uncertain in this description; we assume the issuer does not default.

Price as a present value

Discount every cash flow at one annual rate yyy, using annual compounding as in "Interest, compounding and discounting", and add up the present values. The result is the price PPP:

P=∑t=1TcF(1+y)t+F(1+y)T.P = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{cF}{(1+y)^t} + \frac{F}{(1+y)^T}.P=t=1∑T​(1+y)tcF​+(1+y)TF​.

The coupons form an annuity, so the sum has a closed form:

P=cF 1−(1+y)−Ty+F(1+y)T.P = cF\,\frac{1 - (1+y)^{-T}}{y} + \frac{F}{(1+y)^T}.P=cFy1−(1+y)−T​+(1+y)TF​.

Yield to maturity

In the market the direction is reversed: the price PPP is observed, and yyy is unknown. The yield to maturity (YTM) is the single rate yyy that makes the present value of the cash flows equal to the market price. In the language of investment appraisal it is the internal rate of return of buying the bond at PPP and holding it to maturity.

There is no closed form for yyy when T>1T > 1T>1; it is found numerically (for example by bisection), which is easy because the price is a strictly decreasing function of yyy. The yield is a summary number: it assumes every cash flow is discounted at the same rate.

Yield to maturity: the one discount rate yyy at which the present value of all promised cash flows equals the bond's price.

The price–yield relation

Every term in the price formula has yyy in the denominator, so a higher yield gives a lower price and a lower yield a higher price. The relation is not a straight line. Differentiating each term (1+y)−t(1+y)^{-t}(1+y)−t twice gives a positive result, so the curve y↦P(y)y \mapsto P(y)y↦P(y) is convex: it falls steeply at low yields and flattens at high yields. A rise of 1% in yield therefore lowers the price by less than a fall of 1% raises it.

Premium, par and discount bonds

Compare the coupon rate with the yield.

  • Par: if c=yc = yc=y, the price equals FFF. Each year the coupon pays exactly the interest the market demands, so nothing needs to be made up through the price.
  • Premium: if c>yc > yc>y, the bond pays more than the market rate, and buyers pay more than FFF for it.
  • Discount: if c<yc < yc<y, the coupons are too low, and the bond sells below FFF so that the gain from the price rising to FFF at maturity makes up the shortfall.

Zero-coupon bonds

A zero-coupon bond has c=0c = 0c=0: it pays only FFF at TTT. Its price is a single discounted amount,

P=F(1+y)T,P = \frac{F}{(1+y)^T},P=(1+y)TF​,

so its yield can be solved directly: y=(F/P)1/T−1y = (F/P)^{1/T} - 1y=(F/P)1/T−1. A 10-year zero at y=6%y = 6\%y=6% costs 100/1.0610≈55.84100/1.06^{10} \approx 55.84100/1.0610≈55.84. Zeros always trade at a discount for positive yields, and they are the building blocks of every other bond: a coupon bond is a bundle of zeros, one for each payment date.

Current yield

A simpler number often quoted is the current yield, the annual coupon divided by the price, cF/PcF/PcF/P. It ignores the gain or loss from the price moving to FFF at maturity. For a discount bond the YTM is higher than the current yield, which is in turn higher than the coupon rate; for a premium bond the order is reversed. At par all three coincide.

Pull to par

If the yield stays the same, the price of a bond moves towards FFF as maturity approaches, because fewer coupons are left to be over- or under-priced and the face value is discounted over a shorter time. With c=5%c = 5\%c=5% and y=6%y = 6\%y=6%, the price is about 92.64 with 10 years left, 95.79 with 5 years left and 99.06 with one year left; at maturity it is 100. This is the pull to par. In the figure, shorten TTT at a fixed yield and the curve flattens towards the par line.

Summary

  • A fixed-rate bond is a list of known cash flows: coupons cFcFcF each year and the face value FFF at maturity.
  • Its price is the present value of those cash flows; the yield to maturity is the single rate that reproduces the market price (the bond's IRR).
  • Price and yield move in opposite directions along a convex curve.
  • The bond trades at par when c=yc = yc=y, at a premium when c>yc > yc>y and at a discount when c<yc < yc<y; with an unchanged yield its price is pulled to par as maturity approaches.
