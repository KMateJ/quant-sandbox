Credit risk: default probabilities and credit spreads

Lesson 9 of 10 · 1 min read

Every bond in this track so far was assumed to pay exactly what it promised. A company, unlike a government that borrows in its own currency, can fail to pay. This lesson models that risk with one number per year, the hazard rate, prices a zero-coupon bond that may default, and shows where the extra yield on corporate bonds, the credit spread, comes from. It uses the discounting of "Interest, compounding and discounting", the yields of "Bond prices and yields" and the continuously compounded zero yields of "Spot rates, forward rates and the yield curve", and ends with the credit default swap, a close relative of the contracts in "Interest rate swaps".

Default and the default time

An issuer defaults when it misses a payment it owes. We describe default by a random default time τ\tauτ: the date at which the issuer fails. If τ>T\tau > Tτ>T, the issuer is still paying at TTT and a bond maturing at TTT is repaid in full. If τ≤T\tau \le Tτ≤T, the bond is not repaid in full.

The model rests on three assumptions, stated once here: the risk-free rate rrr is constant and continuously compounded; default is independent of interest rates; and a single parameter, the hazard rate, describes how likely default is.

Hazard rate and survival probability

The hazard rate (or default intensity) λ\lambdaλ is the probability of default per unit of time, given that the issuer has survived so far. Over a short interval dtdtdt,

P(t<τ≤t+dt∣τ>t)=λ dt.\mathbb{P}(t < \tau \le t + dt \mid \tau > t) = \lambda\,dt.P(t<τ≤t+dt∣τ>t)=λdt.

Let Q(t)=P(τ>t)Q(t) = \mathbb{P}(\tau > t)Q(t)=P(τ>t) be the survival probability up to ttt. To survive until t+dtt + dtt+dt, the issuer must survive until ttt and then not default in the next dtdtdt:

Q(t+dt)=Q(t) (1−λ dt)⟹dQdt=−λ Q(t),Q(0)=1.Q(t + dt) = Q(t)\,(1 - \lambda\,dt) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{dQ}{dt} = -\lambda\,Q(t), \qquad Q(0) = 1.Q(t+dt)=Q(t)(1−λdt)⟹dtdQ​=−λQ(t),Q(0)=1.

For a constant λ\lambdaλ the solution is an exponential, and the cumulative default probability is its complement:

Q(T)=e−λT,P(τ≤T)=1−e−λT.Q(T) = e^{-\lambda T}, \qquad \mathbb{P}(\tau \le T) = 1 - e^{-\lambda T}.Q(T)=e−λT,P(τ≤T)=1−e−λT.

This is the same algebra as continuous discounting: survival decays at rate λ\lambdaλ just as a discount factor decays at rate rrr. A fair picture is a light bulb with no memory: whatever its age, the chance it fails in the next hour is the same. For small λT\lambda TλT, 1−e−λT≈λT1 - e^{-\lambda T} \approx \lambda T1−e−λT≈λT, so a hazard rate of 2% means roughly a 2% chance of default per year.

Recovery and loss given default

Default rarely wipes out a bond. Creditors usually recover part of what they are owed from the issuer's remaining assets. The recovery rate RRR is the fraction of face value recovered, and 1−R1 - R1−R is the loss given default. Recovery for senior unsecured corporate bonds is often around 40%, which is why R=40%R = 40\%R=40% is a common default value.

We need to say when the recovery is paid. Here we assume recovery of face paid at maturity: if the issuer defaults before TTT, the holder receives RRR per unit of face at TTT. This keeps the payoff on a single date and makes the price easy to write down.

Pricing a defaultable zero-coupon bond

A risky zero-coupon bond with face 1 maturing at TTT pays 1 if τ>T\tau > Tτ>T and RRR if τ≤T\tau \le Tτ≤T. As in every pricing problem, the price is the discounted expected payoff with probabilities that make prices consistent (the risk-neutral probabilities of "Risk-neutral probabilities"; more on this below). Because default is independent of rates, the discount factor e−rTe^{-rT}e−rT comes out of the expectation:

Prisky=e−rT[ Q(T)⋅1+(1−Q(T))⋅R ]=e−rT[e−λT+(1−e−λT)R].P_{\text{risky}} = e^{-rT}\Big[\,Q(T)\cdot 1 + \big(1 - Q(T)\big)\cdot R\,\Big] = e^{-rT}\Big[e^{-\lambda T} + \big(1 - e^{-\lambda T}\big)R\Big].Prisky​=e−rT[Q(T)⋅1+(1−Q(T))⋅R]=e−rT[e−λT+(1−e−λT)R].

Two checks: with λ=0\lambda = 0λ=0 the bond never defaults and the price is the risk-free e−rTe^{-rT}e−rT; with R=1R = 1R=1 default costs nothing and the price is again e−rTe^{-rT}e−rT.

Credit spread

The continuously compounded yield of the risky zero is defined exactly as in "Spot rates, forward rates and the yield curve": y=−ln⁡Prisky/Ty = -\ln P_{\text{risky}}/Ty=−lnPrisky​/T. The credit spread is the extra yield over the risk-free rate:

s(T)=y−r=−1Tln⁡[e−λT+(1−e−λT)R]=−1Tln⁡[1−(1−R)(1−e−λT)].s(T) = y - r = -\frac{1}{T}\ln\Big[e^{-\lambda T} + \big(1 - e^{-\lambda T}\big)R\Big] = -\frac{1}{T}\ln\Big[1 - (1 - R)\big(1 - e^{-\lambda T}\big)\Big].s(T)=y−r=−T1​ln[e−λT+(1−e−λT)R]=−T1​ln[1−(1−R)(1−e−λT)].

The risk-free rate has dropped out: under these assumptions the spread depends only on λ\lambdaλ, RRR and TTT. If R=0R = 0R=0, the bracket is e−λTe^{-\lambda T}e−λT and the spread is exactly λ\lambdaλ.

The credit triangle

For small λT\lambda TλT, use 1−e−λT≈λT1 - e^{-\lambda T} \approx \lambda T1−e−λT≈λT and ln⁡(1−x)≈−x\ln(1 - x) \approx -xln(1−x)≈−x for small xxx:

s(T)≈−1Tln⁡[1−(1−R)λT]≈(1−R)λTT=λ (1−R).s(T) \approx -\frac{1}{T}\ln\big[1 - (1 - R)\lambda T\big] \approx \frac{(1 - R)\lambda T}{T} = \lambda\,(1 - R).s(T)≈−T1​ln[1−(1−R)λT]≈T(1−R)λT​=λ(1−R).

This is the credit triangle: spread, hazard rate and loss given default, any two of which give the third. In words, the spread pays for the expected loss per year: the chance of default per year times the fraction lost. The approximation is good when λT\lambda TλT is small (a few percent) and exact when R=0R = 0R=0; for high hazard rates or long maturities the exact spread falls below λ(1−R)\lambda(1 - R)λ(1−R).

Credit triangle: s≈λ (1−R)s \approx \lambda\,(1 - R)s≈λ(1−R). The spread is the expected loss rate: hazard rate times loss given default.

Worked example

Take λ=2%\lambda = 2\%λ=2%, R=40%R = 40\%R=40%, r=3%r = 3\%r=3% and T=5T = 5T=5 years.

Spread against maturity

It plots the risk-free zero yield and the risky zero yield against maturity from 0.25 to 10 years. The readouts show the 5-year survival probability, the 5-year credit spread, the CDS premium λ(1−R)\lambda(1 - R)λ(1−R) and the 5-year risky zero-coupon price; at the defaults these are 90.48%, 1.18%, 1.20% and 0.8116. Set RRR to its maximum and note how the gap narrows; a recovery of 100% (the formula's limit) would remove the spread entirely. Raise λ\lambdaλ and the risky curve moves up. Note also that for a constant λ\lambdaλ the spread is nearly flat in maturity, drifting down slightly at long maturities where λT\lambda TλT is no longer small.

Risk-neutral and historical default probabilities

Reading the triangle backwards, a market spread sss and an assumed recovery RRR imply a hazard rate λ=s/(1−R)\lambda = s/(1 - R)λ=s/(1−R). This implied λ\lambdaλ is a risk-neutral default intensity: the one that makes the formula match market prices. It is typically well above the historical default rate observed for similar issuers. The spread is not only expected loss: investors also demand a premium for bearing default risk, which tends to strike in bad times for everyone, and for holding bonds that are harder to sell than government bonds (a liquidity premium). For pricing and hedging, use the implied λ\lambdaλ; for forecasting actual losses, use historical rates.

Credit default swaps

A credit default swap (CDS) is insurance against the default of a reference issuer. The protection buyer pays a fixed premium sCDSs_{\text{CDS}}sCDS​ per year on a notional until maturity or default, whichever comes first. If the issuer defaults, the protection seller pays 1−R1 - R1−R per unit of notional. Like the swaps of "Interest rate swaps", the fair premium sets the present values of the two legs equal. With continuous premium payments, constant λ\lambdaλ and rrr, and the protection paid at the moment of default:

sCDS∫0Te−(r+λ)t dt=(1−R)∫0Tλ e−(r+λ)t dt⟹sCDS=λ (1−R).s_{\text{CDS}}\int_0^T e^{-(r+\lambda)t}\,dt = (1 - R)\int_0^T \lambda\,e^{-(r+\lambda)t}\,dt \quad\Longrightarrow\quad s_{\text{CDS}} = \lambda\,(1 - R).sCDS​∫0T​e−(r+λ)tdt=(1−R)∫0T​λe−(r+λ)tdt⟹sCDS​=λ(1−R).

Here e−λte^{-\lambda t}e−λt is the probability the premium is still being paid at ttt, and λe−λt dt\lambda e^{-\lambda t}\,dtλe−λtdt the probability of default in [t,t+dt][t, t + dt][t,t+dt]. In this setting the triangle holds exactly for the CDS; the bond spread of 1.176% in the example differs slightly because its recovery is paid at maturity rather than at default. In practice, CDS premiums quoted for several maturities are turned into a term structure of hazard rates by bootstrapping, one maturity at a time, just as spot rates were extracted from bond prices.

Summary

  • The hazard rate λ\lambdaλ is the default probability per unit time given survival; for constant λ\lambdaλ the survival probability is e−λTe^{-\lambda T}e−λT.
  • With recovery RRR of face paid at maturity, Prisky=e−rT[e−λT+(1−e−λT)R]P_{\text{risky}} = e^{-rT}[e^{-\lambda T} + (1 - e^{-\lambda T})R]Prisky​=e−rT[e−λT+(1−e−λT)R].
  • The credit spread is approximately λ(1−R)\lambda(1 - R)λ(1−R), accurate when λT\lambda TλT is small; at λ=2%\lambda = 2\%λ=2%, R=40%R = 40\%R=40% the 5-year spread is 1.176% against 1.20%.
  • Implied (risk-neutral) hazard rates exceed historical default rates because spreads also contain risk and liquidity premia.
  • A CDS exchanges a premium for a payment of 1−R1 - R1−R on default; its fair premium is about λ(1−R)\lambda(1 - R)λ(1−R).
