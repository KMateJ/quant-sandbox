Duration and convexity

Lesson 2 of 10 · 1 min read

In "Bond prices and yields" the price of a bond was a decreasing, convex function of its yield. This lesson puts numbers on that curve: duration measures its slope, convexity its curvature. Together they tell you how much a bond's price changes when yields move, without repricing it. The notation is the same: face value F=100F = 100F=100, annual coupons at rate ccc, maturity TTT, yield to maturity yyy with annual compounding, price PPP.

Interest-rate risk

A bond's cash flows are fixed, but its price is not: when market yields rise, the same cash flows are discounted more heavily and the price falls. This exposure is interest-rate risk. Two bonds can react very differently to the same change in yield; a 30-year bond loses far more than a 1-year bill. We want one number that captures this sensitivity.

Macaulay duration

Write CFtCF_tCFt​ for the cash flow at year ttt (so CFt=cFCF_t = cFCFt​=cF for t<Tt < Tt<T and CFT=cF+FCF_T = cF + FCFT​=cF+F). Each cash flow contributes a present value PVt=CFt/(1+y)tPV_t = CF_t/(1+y)^tPVt​=CFt​/(1+y)t to the price, and these contributions add up to PPP. The Macaulay duration is the average payment time, weighted by these present values:

D=∑t=1Tt PVtP,PVt=CFt(1+y)t.D = \sum_{t=1}^{T} t\,\frac{PV_t}{P}, \qquad PV_t = \frac{CF_t}{(1+y)^t}.D=t=1∑T​tPPVt​​,PVt​=(1+y)tCFt​​.

Think of a seesaw: put a weight PVtPV_tPVt​ at distance ttt along a beam; DDD is the point where the beam balances. For a coupon bond the coupons pull the balance point earlier than TTT, so D<TD < TD<T. A zero-coupon bond has only one cash flow, all the weight sits at TTT, and its duration equals its maturity: D=TD = TD=T.

A bond with Dmod=7.7D_{mod} = 7.7Dmod​=7.7 loses about 7.7% of its value when its yield rises by one percentage point. Geometrically, this approximation replaces the price–yield curve by its tangent at the current yield, exactly as delta replaced the option value curve by its tangent in "Delta and hedging".

Dollar duration

Portfolio managers often need the change in money rather than in percent. The dollar duration is Dmod PD_{mod}\,PDmod​P, the price change per unit of yield; divided by 10 000 it gives the change per basis point (0.01%), often called DV01. Dollar durations add across positions, which is how a bond portfolio's interest-rate exposure is aggregated and hedged.

Convexity

The tangent ignores the bend of the curve. Differentiating once more,

d2Pdy2=∑t=1Tt(t+1) CFt(1+y)t+2>0,\frac{d^2P}{dy^2} = \sum_{t=1}^{T} t(t+1)\,\frac{CF_t}{(1+y)^{t+2}} > 0,dy2d2P​=t=1∑T​t(t+1)(1+y)t+2CFt​​>0,

and the convexity is this second derivative per unit of price:

C=1P d2Pdy2.C = \frac{1}{P}\,\frac{d^2P}{dy^2}.C=P1​dy2d2P​.

A second-order Taylor expansion then gives

ΔPP≈−Dmod Δy+12 C (Δy)2.\frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -D_{mod}\,\Delta y + \tfrac{1}{2}\,C\,(\Delta y)^2.PΔP​≈−Dmod​Δy+21​C(Δy)2.

This mirrors the gamma term 12Γ(dS)2\tfrac{1}{2}\Gamma(dS)^221​Γ(dS)2 of the delta hedge. Because C>0C > 0C>0 and (Δy)2≥0(\Delta y)^2 \ge 0(Δy)2≥0, the correction is always positive: when yields fall the bond gains more than duration predicts, and when they rise it loses less. Between two bonds with the same price and duration, the holder prefers the one with higher convexity, and in markets that extra protection is usually paid for with a slightly lower yield. Convexity grows roughly with the square of maturity, so it matters most for long bonds and large yield moves.

Duration as a balance point

Put each cash flow on a beam at its payment date, with a weight equal to its present value. The Macaulay duration is the point where the beam balances, because it is the present-value-weighted average time. In the figure, raise the yield and watch the fulcrum slide towards today: distant payments are discounted more, so they weigh less. Set the coupon to zero and the fulcrum sits exactly at maturity, as for any zero-coupon bond.

Summary

  • Macaulay duration DDD is the present-value-weighted average time of a bond's cash flows; for a zero it equals the maturity.
  • Modified duration Dmod=D/(1+y)D_{mod} = D/(1+y)Dmod​=D/(1+y) gives the first-order price change ΔP/P≈−DmodΔy\Delta P/P \approx -D_{mod}\Delta yΔP/P≈−Dmod​Δy, the tangent to the price–yield curve.
  • Convexity C=P−1 d2P/dy2C = P^{-1}\,d^2P/dy^2C=P−1d2P/dy2 adds the second-order term 12C(Δy)2\tfrac{1}{2}C(\Delta y)^221​C(Δy)2, which is always in the holder's favour.
  • Dollar duration DmodPD_{mod}PDmod​P measures the money at risk per unit of yield and adds across a portfolio.
