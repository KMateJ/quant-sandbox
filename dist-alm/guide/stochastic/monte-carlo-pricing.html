Monte Carlo option pricing

Lesson 13 of 17 · 1 min read

Many quantities in finance are expected values: the price of an option is a discounted expected payoff, a risk measure is a tail probability. When the expectation has no closed form, it can be estimated by Monte Carlo simulation: draw many random outcomes and average them. This lesson explains why the average works, how fast it converges, and how to price a European call this way, using the stock price solution from "Itô's lemma".

Expectations as long-run averages

Let XXX be a random variable whose mean E[X]\mathbb{E}[X]E[X] we want. Draw NNN independent copies X1,…,XNX_1, \dots, X_NX1​,…,XN​ with the same distribution and form the sample mean

μ^N=1N∑i=1NXi.\hat\mu_N = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} X_i.μ^​N​=N1​i=1∑N​Xi​.

The law of large numbers says that μ^N→E[X]\hat\mu_N \to \mathbb{E}[X]μ^​N​→E[X] as N→∞N \to \inftyN→∞. This is the entire idea of Monte Carlo: replace an integral that is hard to compute by an average over simulated outcomes that are easy to generate.

The estimator is unbiased: E[μ^N]=E[X]\mathbb{E}[\hat\mu_N] = \mathbb{E}[X]E[μ^​N​]=E[X] for every NNN. Any single run is off by a random error, but the error has no systematic direction.

Standard error

Let sss be the standard deviation of one draw XiX_iXi​. Since the draws are independent, the variance of their sum is Ns2N s^2Ns2, and dividing by NNN gives

Var⁡(μ^N)=s2N,SE⁡=sd⁡(μ^N)=sN.\operatorname{Var}(\hat\mu_N) = \frac{s^2}{N}, \qquad \operatorname{SE} = \operatorname{sd}(\hat\mu_N) = \frac{s}{\sqrt{N}}.Var(μ^​N​)=Ns2​,SE=sd(μ^​N​)=N​s​.

This is the standard error of the estimate. It falls like 1/N1/\sqrt{N}1/N​, the same square-root law that governed the random walk in "Random walks and Brownian motion". The consequence is sobering: to make the error 10 times smaller you need 100 times as many simulations.

Monte Carlo error: SE⁡=s/N\operatorname{SE} = s/\sqrt{N}SE=s/N​. One extra digit of accuracy costs a hundredfold more draws.

Confidence intervals

By the central limit theorem, for large NNN the estimate μ^N\hat\mu_Nμ^​N​ is approximately normal around the true mean with standard deviation SE⁡\operatorname{SE}SE. The true value sss is unknown, so we use the sample standard deviation of the draws in its place. An approximate 95% confidence interval for E[X]\mathbb{E}[X]E[X] is

μ^N±1.96 sN.\hat\mu_N \pm 1.96\,\frac{s}{\sqrt{N}}.μ^​N​±1.96N​s​.

If the whole experiment were repeated many times, about 95% of such intervals would contain the true mean. Always report a Monte Carlo estimate together with its interval: the estimate alone says nothing about how much to trust it.

Pricing a European call

"Risk-neutral probabilities" showed that a derivative's price is its discounted expected payoff under the measure Q\mathbb{Q}Q. For a European call with strike KKK and maturity TTT,

C0=e−rT EQ[max⁡(ST−K, 0)].C_0 = e^{-rT}\,\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\big[\max(S_T - K,\ 0)\big].C0​=e−rTEQ[max(ST​−K, 0)].

Under Q\mathbb{Q}Q the stock follows GBM with drift rrr. By Itô's lemma its terminal value is exact: ST=S0exp⁡((r−12σ2)T+σWT)S_T = S_0 \exp((r - \tfrac12\sigma^2)T + \sigma W_T)ST​=S0​exp((r−21​σ2)T+σWT​), and WT=T ZW_T = \sqrt{T}\,ZWT​=T​Z with ZZZ standard normal. No time-stepping is needed for a payoff that depends only on STS_TST​. The algorithm is:

  1. Draw NNN independent standard normal numbers Z1,…,ZNZ_1, \dots, Z_NZ1​,…,ZN​.
  2. Compute terminal prices ST(i)=S0exp⁡((r−12σ2)T+σT Zi)S_T^{(i)} = S_0 \exp\big((r - \tfrac12\sigma^2)T + \sigma\sqrt{T}\,Z_i\big)ST(i)​=S0​exp((r−21​σ2)T+σT​Zi​).
  3. Compute payoffs max⁡(ST(i)−K,0)\max(S_T^{(i)} - K, 0)max(ST(i)​−K,0) and discount each by e−rTe^{-rT}e−rT.
  4. Average the discounted payoffs; their sample standard deviation divided by N\sqrt{N}N​ is the standard error.

For a European call the closed form is obviously better. Monte Carlo pays off for payoffs that depend on the whole path (Asian or barrier options), for many underlyings at once, and for models such as Heston where no simple formula exists. The flexibility is the same in all cases; only the payoff function changes.

Variance reduction

Since the error is s/Ns/\sqrt{N}s/N​, there are two ways to improve it: raise NNN, or lower sss. Techniques that lower sss are called variance reduction. The simplest is antithetic variates: for every draw ZZZ also use −Z-Z−Z, and average the two payoffs. The pair moves in opposite directions, so much of their noise cancels. Another is a control variate: simulate alongside a quantity whose mean is known exactly, such as the discounted stock price with mean S0S_0S0​, and correct the estimate by the observed error in that quantity.

Summary

  • Monte Carlo estimates an expectation by the average of NNN independent simulated outcomes; the law of large numbers makes it converge.
  • The standard error is s/Ns/\sqrt{N}s/N​: halving the error needs four times as many draws.
  • A 95% confidence interval is the estimate ±1.96 s/N\pm 1.96\,s/\sqrt{N}±1.96s/N​.
  • A European call is priced by averaging e−rTmax⁡(ST−K,0)e^{-rT}\max(S_T - K, 0)e−rTmax(ST​−K,0) over risk-neutral draws of ST=S0e(r−σ2/2)T+σTZS_T = S_0 e^{(r - \sigma^2/2)T + \sigma\sqrt{T}Z}ST​=S0​e(r−σ2/2)T+σT​Z; the result converges to Black–Scholes.
  • Its real strength is flexibility: path-dependent payoffs and models without closed forms.
