Correlated Brownian motions and multi-asset models

Lesson 15 of 17 · 1 min read

Everything so far used one Brownian motion at a time. Real models rarely do. A stock and its volatility move together, a basket option depends on several assets at once, and a portfolio is exactly the situation of "Diversification and correlation". This lesson builds two Brownian motions with a chosen correlation, from the properties in "Properties of Brownian motion", shows how the same construction extends to many assets, and states the two-dimensional version of the rule in "Itô's lemma". It also says where correlation enters pricing models and how it is simulated with the scheme of "Simulating SDEs: the Euler–Maruyama scheme".

Two Brownian motions with correlation ρ

Two standard Brownian motions W1W_1W1​ and W2W_2W2​ have correlation ρ∈[−1,1]\rho \in [-1, 1]ρ∈[−1,1] if their increments over a short interval dtdtdt have correlation ρ\rhoρ. In the language of quadratic variation this is a single rule for the cross term:

d⟨W1,W2⟩t=ρ dt,d⟨Wi⟩t=dt.d\langle W_1, W_2 \rangle_t = \rho\,dt, \qquad d\langle W_i \rangle_t = dt.d⟨W1​,W2​⟩t​=ρdt,d⟨Wi​⟩t​=dt.

It is easy to build such a pair from two independent Brownian motions W1W_1W1​ and W⊥W_\perpW⊥​. Keep W1W_1W1​ and mix the other in:

W2=ρ W1+1−ρ2  W⊥.W_2 = \rho\,W_1 + \sqrt{1 - \rho^2}\;W_\perp.W2​=ρW1​+1−ρ2​W⊥​.

Then W2W_2W2​ has independent normal increments with variance (ρ2+1−ρ2) dt=dt(\rho^2 + 1 - \rho^2)\,dt = dt(ρ2+1−ρ2)dt=dt, so it is a standard Brownian motion in its own right, and its increment covaries with that of W1W_1W1​ by ρ dt\rho\,dtρdt. At ρ=0\rho = 0ρ=0 the two are independent. At ρ=±1\rho = \pm 1ρ=±1 they are the same path or its mirror image. The figure draws both paths from one set of random shocks and lets you move ρ\rhoρ. Start at the default ρ=−0.7\rho = -0.7ρ=−0.7 and watch the paths mostly mirror each other; the sample correlation of the increments is noisy for a path with only 250 steps, but the construction fixes the exact value of ⟨W1,W2⟩T=ρ T\langle W_1, W_2 \rangle_T = \rho\,T⟨W1​,W2​⟩T​=ρT, which is −0.70-0.70−0.70 for T=1T = 1T=1.

The Cholesky factor

The construction above is a matrix product. Write the independent shocks of one step as a vector ε=(ε1,ε2)\varepsilon = (\varepsilon_1, \varepsilon_2)ε=(ε1​,ε2​) of standard normals and the correlated ones as Z=L εZ = L\,\varepsilonZ=Lε with

L=(10ρ1−ρ2),LL⊤=(1ρρ1).L = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ \rho & \sqrt{1 - \rho^2} \end{pmatrix}, \qquad L L^{\top} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \rho \\ \rho & 1 \end{pmatrix}.L=(1ρ​01−ρ2​​),LL⊤=(1ρ​ρ1​).

This LLL is the Cholesky factor of the correlation matrix. For nnn assets with correlation matrix CCC, the same idea works: find a lower-triangular LLL with LL⊤=CL L^{\top} = CLL⊤=C and set Z=L εZ = L\,\varepsilonZ=Lε for independent ε\varepsilonε. Such an LLL exists exactly when CCC is positive definite, which is a real restriction. A table of pairwise correlations chosen independently, say ρ12=ρ13=0.9\rho_{12} = \rho_{13} = 0.9ρ12​=ρ13​=0.9 and ρ23=−0.9\rho_{23} = -0.9ρ23​=−0.9, can fail it, and then no set of random variables has those correlations. The algorithm reports the failure when a square root of a non-positive number would be needed; the figure's readout 1−ρ2\sqrt{1 - \rho^2}1−ρ2​ is that square root for two assets, and it vanishes at ρ=±1\rho = \pm 1ρ=±1.

The two-dimensional Itô rule

Let dX=μ1 dt+σ1 dW1dX = \mu_1\,dt + \sigma_1\,dW_1dX=μ1​dt+σ1​dW1​ and dY=μ2 dt+σ2 dW2dY = \mu_2\,dt + \sigma_2\,dW_2dY=μ2​dt+σ2​dW2​ with d⟨W1,W2⟩=ρ dtd\langle W_1, W_2 \rangle = \rho\,dtd⟨W1​,W2​⟩=ρdt. For a smooth function f(t,x,y)f(t, x, y)f(t,x,y), "Itô's lemma" extends by one extra term, the cross term, because dX dY=ρ σ1σ2 dtdX\,dY = \rho\,\sigma_1 \sigma_2\,dtdXdY=ρσ1​σ2​dt:

df=ft dt+fx dX+fy dY+12fxx σ12 dt+12fyy σ22 dt+fxy ρ σ1σ2 dt.df = f_t\,dt + f_x\,dX + f_y\,dY + \tfrac{1}{2} f_{xx}\,\sigma_1^2\,dt + \tfrac{1}{2} f_{yy}\,\sigma_2^2\,dt + f_{xy}\,\rho\,\sigma_1 \sigma_2\,dt.df=ft​dt+fx​dX+fy​dY+21​fxx​σ12​dt+21​fyy​σ22​dt+fxy​ρσ1​σ2​dt.

With f(x,y)=x yf(x, y) = x\,yf(x,y)=xy this is the product rule for correlated processes:

d(XY)=X dY+Y dX+ρ σ1σ2 dt.d(XY) = X\,dY + Y\,dX + \rho\,\sigma_1 \sigma_2\,dt.d(XY)=XdY+YdX+ρσ1​σ2​dt.

For two Brownian motions themselves (X=W1X = W_1X=W1​, Y=W2Y = W_2Y=W2​, σ1=σ2=1\sigma_1 = \sigma_2 = 1σ1​=σ2​=1) the extra term is ρ dt\rho\,dtρdt. The product W1W2W_1 W_2W1​W2​ is therefore not a martingale; its drift is ρ\rhoρ, and taking expectations gives E[W1(T) W2(T)]=ρ TE[W_1(T)\,W_2(T)] = \rho\,TE[W1​(T)W2​(T)]=ρT.

Where correlation enters

In the Heston model of "Stochastic volatility and the Heston model", the price and the variance are driven by Brownian motions with correlation ρ\rhoρ:

dS=μS dt+v S dW1,dv=κ(θ−v) dt+ξv dW2,d⟨W1,W2⟩=ρ dt.dS = \mu S\,dt + \sqrt{v}\,S\,dW_1, \qquad dv = \kappa(\theta - v)\,dt + \xi\sqrt{v}\,dW_2, \qquad d\langle W_1, W_2 \rangle = \rho\,dt.dS=μSdt+v​SdW1​,dv=κ(θ−v)dt+ξv​dW2​,d⟨W1​,W2​⟩=ρdt.

Equity indices fall when volatility rises, so the calibrated ρ\rhoρ is negative, typically between −0.5-0.5−0.5 and −0.9-0.9−0.9. That negative sign is what tilts the implied volatility smile into a skew. Correlation also drives basket and spread options, whose payoff depends on several assets: the variance of a weighted sum includes 2 wiwj ρij σiσj2\,w_i w_j\,\rho_{ij}\,\sigma_i \sigma_j2wi​wj​ρij​σi​σj​ terms, exactly as the portfolio variance does. A copula would let the dependence differ from this linear one; we do not need it here.

Simulating correlated assets

In the Euler scheme the only change is the shock. At each step draw independent standard normals ε1,ε2\varepsilon_1, \varepsilon_2ε1​,ε2​ and use Z1=ε1Z_1 = \varepsilon_1Z1​=ε1​, Z2=ρ ε1+1−ρ2 ε2Z_2 = \rho\,\varepsilon_1 + \sqrt{1 - \rho^2}\,\varepsilon_2Z2​=ρε1​+1−ρ2​ε2​ in place of the two independent shocks. Everything else, including the pricing average of "Monte Carlo option pricing", stays as it was.

Worked example

Correlated shocks for ρ = −0.7, and the product rule

  1. The weight of the independent shock is the second Cholesky entry.

    L22=1−(−0.7)2\displaystyle L_{22} = \sqrt{1 - (-0.7)^2}L22​=1−(−0.7)2​=0.7141

  2. Draw ε1=0.8\varepsilon_1 = 0.8ε1​=0.8 and ε2=−0.3\varepsilon_2 = -0.3ε2​=−0.3. The first correlated shock is Z1=ε1=0.8Z_1 = \varepsilon_1 = 0.8Z1​=ε1​=0.8; the second mixes both.

    Z2=ρ ε1+L22 ε2=−0.7×0.8+0.7141×(−0.3)\displaystyle Z_2 = \rho\,\varepsilon_1 + L_{22}\,\varepsilon_2 = -0.7 \times 0.8 + 0.7141 \times (-0.3)Z2​=ρε1​+L22​ε2​=−0.7×0.8+0.7141×(−0.3)=-0.7742

  3. The product rule gives d(W1W2)=W2 dW1+W1 dW2+ρ dtd(W_1 W_2) = W_2\,dW_1 + W_1\,dW_2 + \rho\,dtd(W1​W2​)=W2​dW1​+W1​dW2​+ρdt. The two stochastic integrals have mean zero, so the expected product after T=1T = 1T=1 is ρT\rho TρT.

    E[W1(1) W2(1)]=ρ⋅1\displaystyle E[W_1(1)\,W_2(1)] = \rho \cdot 1E[W1​(1)W2​(1)]=ρ⋅1=-0.7

Summary

  • Two Brownian motions have correlation ρ\rhoρ when d⟨W1,W2⟩=ρ dtd\langle W_1, W_2 \rangle = \rho\,dtd⟨W1​,W2​⟩=ρdt; build them as W2=ρW1+1−ρ2 W⊥W_2 = \rho W_1 + \sqrt{1 - \rho^2}\,W_\perpW2​=ρW1​+1−ρ2​W⊥​.
  • For nnn assets, correlated shocks are Z=LεZ = L\varepsilonZ=Lε, where LL⊤=CL L^{\top} = CLL⊤=C is the Cholesky factor, which exists exactly when CCC is positive definite.
  • The two-dimensional Itô rule adds the cross term fxy ρ σ1σ2 dtf_{xy}\,\rho\,\sigma_1 \sigma_2\,dtfxy​ρσ1​σ2​dt, which gives d(XY)=X dY+Y dX+ρ σ1σ2 dtd(XY) = X\,dY + Y\,dX + \rho\,\sigma_1 \sigma_2\,dtd(XY)=XdY+YdX+ρσ1​σ2​dt.
  • Correlation drives the skew in Heston (with ρ<0\rho < 0ρ<0 for equities) and the price of basket and spread options.
