Kolmogorov equations and the Feynman–Kac formula

Lesson 11 of 17 · 1 min read

A price can be written as a discounted expectation, as in "Change of measure and Girsanov's theorem", or as the solution of a partial differential equation. This lesson proves that the two descriptions agree. It uses Itô's formula from "Itô's lemma", the martingale property of the integral from "The Itô integral" and conditional expectations from "Conditional expectation and martingales".

Diffusions and their generator

Let XXX solve the stochastic differential equation

dXs=μ(Xs) ds+σ(Xs) dWs,dX_s = \mu(X_s)\,ds + \sigma(X_s)\,dW_s,dXs​=μ(Xs​)ds+σ(Xs​)dWs​,

where WWW is a Brownian motion and the coefficients μ,σ:R→R\mu, \sigma : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}μ,σ:R→R are Lipschitz continuous. Under this assumption the equation has a unique solution for every starting point, and XXX is a Markov process: given XtX_tXt​, the future of XXX does not depend on the earlier path. Write Et,x[ ⋅ ]=E[ ⋅∣Xt=x]\mathbb{E}_{t,x}[\,\cdot\,] = \mathbb{E}[\,\cdot \mid X_t = x]Et,x​[⋅]=E[⋅∣Xt​=x].

The generator of XXX is the operator that gives the expected rate of change of f(X)f(X)f(X) at a point. For twice continuously differentiable fff with compact support,

Lf(x)=lim⁡h↓0E0,x[f(Xh)]−f(x)h.\mathcal{L}f(x) = \lim_{h \downarrow 0} \frac{\mathbb{E}_{0,x}[f(X_h)] - f(x)}{h}.Lf(x)=h↓0lim​hE0,x​[f(Xh​)]−f(x)​.

Itô's lemma computes it. Integrating df(Xs)df(X_s)df(Xs​) over [0,h][0, h][0,h],

f(Xh)=f(x)+∫0h(μf′+12σ2f′′)(Xs) ds+∫0hσ(Xs)f′(Xs) dWs.f(X_h) = f(x) + \int_0^h \Big(\mu f' + \tfrac12 \sigma^2 f''\Big)(X_s)\,ds + \int_0^h \sigma(X_s) f'(X_s)\,dW_s.f(Xh​)=f(x)+∫0h​(μf′+21​σ2f′′)(Xs​)ds+∫0h​σ(Xs​)f′(Xs​)dWs​.

The integrand σf′\sigma f'σf′ is bounded on the support of fff, so the Itô integral has mean zero. Taking expectations, dividing by hhh and letting h→0h \to 0h→0 (the dsdsds-integrand is continuous) gives

Lf(x)=μ(x) f′(x)+12 σ2(x) f′′(x).\mathcal{L}f(x) = \mu(x)\,f'(x) + \tfrac12\,\sigma^2(x)\,f''(x).Lf(x)=μ(x)f′(x)+21​σ2(x)f′′(x).

The drift contributes a first-derivative term and the diffusion a second-derivative term; the 12σ2\tfrac12\sigma^221​σ2 is the Itô correction. Examples: Brownian motion with volatility σ\sigmaσ has L=12σ2 ∂xx\mathcal{L} = \tfrac12\sigma^2\,\partial_{xx}L=21​σ2∂xx​; geometric Brownian motion under Q\mathbb{Q}Q has Lf=rx f′+12σ2x2f′′\mathcal{L}f = rx\,f' + \tfrac12\sigma^2 x^2 f''Lf=rxf′+21​σ2x2f′′. The same computation without the limit gives Dynkin's formula, E0,x[f(Xt)]=f(x)+E0,x[∫0tLf(Xs) ds]\mathbb{E}_{0,x}[f(X_t)] = f(x) + \mathbb{E}_{0,x}\big[\int_0^t \mathcal{L}f(X_s)\,ds\big]E0,x​[f(Xt​)]=f(x)+E0,x​[∫0t​Lf(Xs​)ds].

The Kolmogorov backward equation

Fix a horizon TTT and a payoff function ggg, and define v(t,x)=Et,x[g(XT)]v(t, x) = \mathbb{E}_{t,x}[g(X_T)]v(t,x)=Et,x​[g(XT​)]. By the Markov property, v(s,Xs)=E[g(XT)∣Fs]v(s, X_s) = \mathbb{E}[g(X_T) \mid \mathcal{F}_s]v(s,Xs​)=E[g(XT​)∣Fs​] for t≤s≤Tt \le s \le Tt≤s≤T, so s↦v(s,Xs)s \mapsto v(s, X_s)s↦v(s,Xs​) is a martingale. If vvv is once differentiable in ttt and twice in xxx, Itô's lemma gives

dv(s,Xs)=(∂sv+Lv)(s,Xs) ds+σ(Xs) ∂xv(s,Xs) dWs.dv(s, X_s) = \big(\partial_s v + \mathcal{L}v\big)(s, X_s)\,ds + \sigma(X_s)\,\partial_x v(s, X_s)\,dW_s.dv(s,Xs​)=(∂s​v+Lv)(s,Xs​)ds+σ(Xs​)∂x​v(s,Xs​)dWs​.

A continuous martingale whose dsdsds-part is not identically zero cannot exist, since a process of finite variation that is also a continuous martingale is constant. So the dsdsds-term must vanish, which gives the Kolmogorov backward equation

∂tv+Lv=0on [0,T)×R,v(T,x)=g(x).\partial_t v + \mathcal{L}v = 0 \quad \text{on } [0, T) \times \mathbb{R}, \qquad v(T, x) = g(x).∂t​v+Lv=0on [0,T)×R,v(T,x)=g(x).

It runs backwards: the terminal condition is given at TTT and the equation is solved towards t=0t = 0t=0. If XXX has a transition density p(t,x;T,y)p(t, x; T, y)p(t,x;T,y), that density solves the backward equation in (t,x)(t, x)(t,x), and in the forward variables (T,y)(T, y)(T,y) it solves the Kolmogorov forward (Fokker–Planck) equation

∂Tp=−∂y(μ(y) p)+12 ∂yy(σ2(y) p).\partial_T p = -\partial_y\big(\mu(y)\,p\big) + \tfrac12\,\partial_{yy}\big(\sigma^2(y)\,p\big).∂T​p=−∂y​(μ(y)p)+21​∂yy​(σ2(y)p).

The backward equation describes how an expected payoff depends on the starting point; the forward equation describes how probability mass spreads out from a fixed starting point.

The Feynman–Kac theorem

Add a discount rate r(x)≥0r(x) \ge 0r(x)≥0, continuous. Theorem (Feynman–Kac). Let μ,σ\mu, \sigmaμ,σ be Lipschitz and let u(t,x)u(t, x)u(t,x), once continuously differentiable in ttt and twice in xxx, solve

∂tu+μ(x) ∂xu+12σ2(x) ∂xxu−r(x) u=0on [0,T)×R,u(T,x)=g(x).\partial_t u + \mu(x)\,\partial_x u + \tfrac12\sigma^2(x)\,\partial_{xx} u - r(x)\,u = 0 \quad \text{on } [0, T) \times \mathbb{R}, \qquad u(T, x) = g(x).∂t​u+μ(x)∂x​u+21​σ2(x)∂xx​u−r(x)u=0on [0,T)×R,u(T,x)=g(x).

Assume also that Et,x[∫tT(σ(Xs) ∂xu(s,Xs))2ds]<∞\mathbb{E}_{t,x}\big[\int_t^T \big(\sigma(X_s)\,\partial_x u(s, X_s)\big)^2 ds\big] < \inftyEt,x​[∫tT​(σ(Xs​)∂x​u(s,Xs​))2ds]<∞, which holds, for example, if σ ∂xu\sigma\,\partial_x uσ∂x​u is bounded. Then

u(t,x)=Et,x ⁣[e−∫tTr(Xs) ds g(XT)].u(t, x) = \mathbb{E}_{t,x}\!\left[ e^{-\int_t^T r(X_s)\,ds}\, g(X_T) \right].u(t,x)=Et,x​[e−∫tT​r(Xs​)dsg(XT​)].

Proof. Fix (t,x)(t, x)(t,x) and set Ds=e−∫tsr(Xv) dvD_s = e^{-\int_t^s r(X_v)\,dv}Ds​=e−∫ts​r(Xv​)dv and Ys=Ds u(s,Xs)Y_s = D_s\,u(s, X_s)Ys​=Ds​u(s,Xs​) for s∈[t,T]s \in [t, T]s∈[t,T]. The factor DDD has finite variation, dDs=−r(Xs) Ds dsdD_s = -r(X_s)\,D_s\,dsdDs​=−r(Xs​)Ds​ds, so the product rule has no cross term. With Itô's lemma for u(s,Xs)u(s, X_s)u(s,Xs​),

dYs=Ds(∂su+Lu−ru)(s,Xs) ds+Ds σ(Xs) ∂xu(s,Xs) dWs=Ds σ(Xs) ∂xu(s,Xs) dWs.dY_s = D_s\big(\partial_s u + \mathcal{L}u - r u\big)(s, X_s)\,ds + D_s\,\sigma(X_s)\,\partial_x u(s, X_s)\,dW_s = D_s\,\sigma(X_s)\,\partial_x u(s, X_s)\,dW_s.dYs​=Ds​(∂s​u+Lu−ru)(s,Xs​)ds+Ds​σ(Xs​)∂x​u(s,Xs​)dWs​=Ds​σ(Xs​)∂x​u(s,Xs​)dWs​.

The dsdsds-term is zero because uuu solves the equation. Since 0<Ds≤10 < D_s \le 10<Ds​≤1, the integrability assumption makes YYY an Itô integral of an L2\mathcal{L}^2L2 integrand, hence a martingale. Therefore u(t,x)=Yt=Et,x[YT]=Et,x[DT g(XT)]u(t, x) = Y_t = \mathbb{E}_{t,x}[Y_T] = \mathbb{E}_{t,x}\big[D_T\,g(X_T)\big]u(t,x)=Yt​=Et,x​[YT​]=Et,x​[DT​g(XT​)], using u(T,⋅)=gu(T, \cdot) = gu(T,⋅)=g. □\square□

The proof also shows uniqueness: two solutions in the integrability class equal the same expectation. The converse direction, that the expectation is smooth enough to solve the equation, needs more, for example smooth coefficients and σ\sigmaσ bounded away from zero; under such conditions the expectation is the solution. Think of u(s,Xs)u(s, X_s)u(s,Xs​) as a fair value carried along the path: discounted, it neither drifts up nor down, so its value today is the expected discounted value at maturity.

The proof is Itô's lemma plus the fact that a dWdWdW-integral is a martingale.

The Black–Scholes equation

By "Change of measure and Girsanov's theorem", under Q\mathbb{Q}Q the stock follows dSs=rSs ds+σSs dW~sdS_s = rS_s\,ds + \sigma S_s\,d\tilde W_sdSs​=rSs​ds+σSs​dW~s​ with constant rrr and σ\sigmaσ, and the price of a payoff g(ST)g(S_T)g(ST​) is V(t,S)=Et,SQ[e−r(T−t)g(ST)]V(t, S) = \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}_{t,S}\big[e^{-r(T - t)} g(S_T)\big]V(t,S)=Et,SQ​[e−r(T−t)g(ST​)]. This is a Feynman–Kac expectation with μ(S)=rS\mu(S) = rSμ(S)=rS, σ(S)=σS\sigma(S) = \sigma Sσ(S)=σS and constant discount rate rrr, so VVV solves the Black–Scholes equation

∂V∂t+rS ∂V∂S+12σ2S2 ∂2V∂S2−rV=0,V(T,S)=g(S).\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + rS\,\frac{\partial V}{\partial S} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2\,\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} - rV = 0, \qquad V(T, S) = g(S).∂t∂V​+rS∂S∂V​+21​σ2S2∂S2∂2V​−rV=0,V(T,S)=g(S).

Two ways to compute one number

Feynman–Kac gives two routes to the same price. The Monte Carlo route simulates many paths of XXX under Q\mathbb{Q}Q, averages the discounted payoffs and reports a standard error that falls like 1/N1/\sqrt{N}1/N​ in the number of paths, whatever the dimension of XXX. The PDE route discretises xxx and ttt on a grid and steps the equation backwards from u(T,⋅)=gu(T, \cdot) = gu(T,⋅)=g with finite differences; one solve gives uuu at every grid point, and the Greeks come from differences of neighbouring values, but the grid grows exponentially with the dimension of XXX. In practice PDE methods dominate for one to three state variables and Monte Carlo for more. Any difference between the two answers is numerical error, sampling error on one side and grid error on the other, not a difference in the model.
