Properties of Brownian motion

Lesson 6 of 17 · 1 min read

The lesson "Random walks and Brownian motion" obtained Brownian motion as the limit of a scaled coin-flip walk and listed its defining properties informally. With the language of "Probability spaces, σ-algebras and random variables" and "Filtrations, adapted processes and stopping times" in hand, this lesson states the definition precisely, proves that Brownian motion is characterised by its covariance function, and uses that characterisation to derive four invariances, among them time inversion. It ends with the main path properties: recurrence, nowhere differentiability, infinite total variation, finite quadratic variation, and the Markov and strong Markov properties.

Definition of Brownian motion

Let (Ω,F,P)(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})(Ω,F,P) be a probability space. A standard Brownian motion is a family of random variables (Wt)t≥0(W_t)_{t \ge 0}(Wt​)t≥0​ on it such that

  • W0=0W_0 = 0W0​=0 almost surely;
  • for all 0≤t0<t1<⋯<tn0 \le t_0 < t_1 < \cdots < t_n0≤t0​<t1​<⋯<tn​ the increments Wt1−Wt0,…,Wtn−Wtn−1W_{t_1} - W_{t_0}, \dots, W_{t_n} - W_{t_{n-1}}Wt1​​−Wt0​​,…,Wtn​​−Wtn−1​​ are independent;
  • for 0≤s<t0 \le s < t0≤s<t the increment Wt−WsW_t - W_sWt​−Ws​ has the normal distribution N(0,t−s)N(0, t - s)N(0,t−s);
  • for almost every ω∈Ω\omega \in \Omegaω∈Ω the path t↦Wt(ω)t \mapsto W_t(\omega)t↦Wt​(ω) is continuous.

That such a process exists is a theorem (Wiener, 1923); a standard proof builds the finite-dimensional distributions and then uses Kolmogorov's continuity theorem to obtain a continuous version. Given a filtration (Ft)t≥0(\mathcal{F}_t)_{t \ge 0}(Ft​)t≥0​, WWW is a Brownian motion with respect to (Ft)(\mathcal{F}_t)(Ft​) if it is adapted and Wt−WsW_t - W_sWt​−Ws​ is independent of Fs\mathcal{F}_sFs​ for every s<ts < ts<t. For the natural filtration FtW=σ(Wu:u≤t)\mathcal{F}^W_t = \sigma(W_u : u \le t)FtW​=σ(Wu​:u≤t) this follows from the independent increments.

Brownian motion as a Gaussian process

A process (Xt)(X_t)(Xt​) is a Gaussian process if every vector (Xt1,…,Xtn)(X_{t_1}, \dots, X_{t_n})(Xt1​​,…,Xtn​​) is multivariate normal. Its law is then fixed by the mean function E[Xt]\mathbb{E}[X_t]E[Xt​] and the covariance function Cov⁡(Xs,Xt)\operatorname{Cov}(X_s, X_t)Cov(Xs​,Xt​), because a multivariate normal distribution is fixed by its mean vector and covariance matrix.

Brownian motion is Gaussian: (Wt1,…,Wtn)(W_{t_1}, \dots, W_{t_n})(Wt1​​,…,Wtn​​) is a linear transformation of the independent normal increments, since Wtk=∑j≤k(Wtj−Wtj−1)W_{t_k} = \sum_{j \le k}(W_{t_j} - W_{t_{j-1}})Wtk​​=∑j≤k​(Wtj​​−Wtj−1​​). Its mean is zero, and for s≤ts \le ts≤t, splitting Wt=Ws+(Wt−Ws)W_t = W_s + (W_t - W_s)Wt​=Ws​+(Wt​−Ws​) with the second term independent of the first,

Cov⁡(Ws,Wt)=Var⁡(Ws)+Cov⁡(Ws,Wt−Ws)=s=min⁡(s,t).\operatorname{Cov}(W_s, W_t) = \operatorname{Var}(W_s) + \operatorname{Cov}(W_s, W_t - W_s) = s = \min(s, t).Cov(Ws​,Wt​)=Var(Ws​)+Cov(Ws​,Wt​−Ws​)=s=min(s,t).

Characterisation: a process (Xt)t≥0(X_t)_{t \ge 0}(Xt​)t≥0​ with continuous paths is a standard Brownian motion if and only if it is a centred Gaussian process with Cov⁡(Xs,Xt)=min⁡(s,t)\operatorname{Cov}(X_s, X_t) = \min(s, t)Cov(Xs​,Xt​)=min(s,t).

The forward direction was just shown. Conversely, let XXX be such a process. Then Var⁡(X0)=0\operatorname{Var}(X_0) = 0Var(X0​)=0, so X0=0X_0 = 0X0​=0. For s<ts < ts<t, Var⁡(Xt−Xs)=t−2s+s=t−s\operatorname{Var}(X_t - X_s) = t - 2s + s = t - sVar(Xt​−Xs​)=t−2s+s=t−s. For disjoint intervals s<t≤u<vs < t \le u < vs<t≤u<v,

Cov⁡(Xt−Xs, Xv−Xu)=min⁡(t,v)−min⁡(t,u)−min⁡(s,v)+min⁡(s,u)=t−t−s+s=0.\operatorname{Cov}(X_t - X_s,\, X_v - X_u) = \min(t,v) - \min(t,u) - \min(s,v) + \min(s,u) = t - t - s + s = 0.Cov(Xt​−Xs​,Xv​−Xu​)=min(t,v)−min(t,u)−min(s,v)+min(s,u)=t−t−s+s=0.

The increments are jointly normal, and for jointly normal variables zero covariance implies independence. So all four defining properties hold. In practice this means: to show that a transformed process is a Brownian motion, it suffices to check that it is centred Gaussian, has covariance min⁡(s,t)\min(s,t)min(s,t), and has continuous paths.

Four invariances

Each transformation below is a linear map applied to WWW, so the result is again a centred Gaussian process; only the covariance and continuity need checking. Let c>0c > 0c>0 and s≥0s \ge 0s≥0 be fixed.

  • Symmetry. Xt=−WtX_t = -W_tXt​=−Wt​. Then Cov⁡(Xu,Xv)=min⁡(u,v)\operatorname{Cov}(X_u, X_v) = \min(u,v)Cov(Xu​,Xv​)=min(u,v) trivially.
  • Scaling. Xt=c−1/2WctX_t = c^{-1/2} W_{ct}Xt​=c−1/2Wct​. Then Cov⁡(Xu,Xv)=c−1min⁡(cu,cv)=min⁡(u,v)\operatorname{Cov}(X_u, X_v) = c^{-1}\min(cu, cv) = \min(u,v)Cov(Xu​,Xv​)=c−1min(cu,cv)=min(u,v). Speeding up time by ccc and shrinking space by c\sqrt{c}c​ gives the same process.
  • Time shift. Xt=Ws+t−WsX_t = W_{s+t} - W_sXt​=Ws+t​−Ws​. Then Cov⁡(Xu,Xv)=min⁡(s+u,s+v)−s−s+s=min⁡(u,v)\operatorname{Cov}(X_u, X_v) = \min(s+u, s+v) - s - s + s = \min(u,v)Cov(Xu​,Xv​)=min(s+u,s+v)−s−s+s=min(u,v). Moreover XXX is independent of Fs\mathcal{F}_sFs​, because its finite-dimensional vectors are built from increments after sss. This is the simple Markov property: after any fixed time the process starts afresh, independently of the past.
  • Time inversion. Xt=t W1/tX_t = t\,W_{1/t}Xt​=tW1/t​ for t>0t > 0t>0 and X0=0X_0 = 0X0​=0. For u,v>0u, v > 0u,v>0, Cov⁡(Xu,Xv)=uvmin⁡(1/u,1/v)=uv/max⁡(u,v)=min⁡(u,v)\operatorname{Cov}(X_u, X_v) = uv\min(1/u, 1/v) = uv/\max(u,v) = \min(u,v)Cov(Xu​,Xv​)=uvmin(1/u,1/v)=uv/max(u,v)=min(u,v).

Only time inversion needs care with continuity. On (0,∞)(0, \infty)(0,∞) the path t↦tW1/tt \mapsto t W_{1/t}t↦tW1/t​ is continuous; the question is whether Xt→0X_t \to 0Xt​→0 as t↓0t \downarrow 0t↓0. Restricted to the positive rationals, XXX and WWW have the same finite-dimensional distributions, and the event {Xq→0 as q↓0, q∈Q}\{X_q \to 0 \text{ as } q \downarrow 0,\ q \in \mathbb{Q}\}{Xq​→0 as q↓0, q∈Q} is determined by countably many coordinates. Its probability is therefore the same as for WWW, namely 1. Continuity on (0,∞)(0, \infty)(0,∞) then gives Xt→0X_t \to 0Xt​→0 along all ttt. Substituting u=1/tu = 1/tu=1/t yields the law of large numbers for Brownian motion:

lim⁡u→∞Wuu=lim⁡t↓0t W1/t=0almost surely.\lim_{u \to \infty} \frac{W_u}{u} = \lim_{t \downarrow 0} t\,W_{1/t} = 0 \quad \text{almost surely}.u→∞lim​uWu​​=t↓0lim​tW1/t​=0almost surely.

Time inversion swaps the behaviour of the path near t=0t = 0t=0 with its behaviour as t→∞t \to \inftyt→∞: every statement about one end gives a statement about the other.

Path properties

The invariances lead to the following almost-sure properties. They are stated as theorems; the proofs are in any text on Brownian motion.

  • Law of the iterated logarithm (Khinchin): lim sup⁡t→∞Wt/2tln⁡ln⁡t=1\limsup_{t \to \infty} W_t / \sqrt{2t\ln\ln t} = 1limsupt→∞​Wt​/2tlnlnt​=1. By time inversion, also lim sup⁡t↓0Wt/2tln⁡ln⁡(1/t)=1\limsup_{t \downarrow 0} W_t / \sqrt{2t\ln\ln(1/t)} = 1limsupt↓0​Wt​/2tlnln(1/t)​=1.
  • Recurrence: by the law of the iterated logarithm and symmetry, lim sup⁡t→∞Wt=+∞\limsup_{t\to\infty} W_t = +\inftylimsupt→∞​Wt​=+∞ and lim inf⁡t→∞Wt=−∞\liminf_{t\to\infty} W_t = -\inftyliminft→∞​Wt​=−∞. Since paths are continuous, WWW hits every level a∈Ra \in \mathbb{R}a∈R, and does so at arbitrarily large times. Near t=0t = 0t=0 the small-time version shows that WWW returns to 0 infinitely often in every interval [0,ε][0, \varepsilon][0,ε].
  • Nowhere differentiability (Paley, Wiener and Zygmund, 1933): with probability one, t↦Wtt \mapsto W_tt↦Wt​ is differentiable at no ttt. Scaling explains why: (Wt+h−Wt)/h∼N(0,1/h)(W_{t+h} - W_t)/h \sim N(0, 1/h)(Wt+h​−Wt​)/h∼N(0,1/h), so for any bound KKK, P(∣(Wt+h−Wt)/h∣≤K)=P(∣Z∣≤Kh)→0\mathbb{P}(|(W_{t+h} - W_t)/h| \le K) = \mathbb{P}(|Z| \le K\sqrt{h}) \to 0P(∣(Wt+h​−Wt​)/h∣≤K)=P(∣Z∣≤Kh​)→0 as h↓0h \downarrow 0h↓0.

Quadratic and total variation

Take partitions 0=t0<t1<⋯<tn=t0 = t_0 < t_1 < \cdots < t_n = t0=t0​<t1​<⋯<tn​=t with mesh δn=max⁡i(ti+1−ti)→0\delta_n = \max_i (t_{i+1} - t_i) \to 0δn​=maxi​(ti+1​−ti​)→0, write ΔWi=Wti+1−Wti\Delta W_i = W_{t_{i+1}} - W_{t_i}ΔWi​=Wti+1​​−Wti​​, Δti=ti+1−ti\Delta t_i = t_{i+1} - t_iΔti​=ti+1​−ti​, and Qn=∑i(ΔWi)2Q_n = \sum_i (\Delta W_i)^2Qn​=∑i​(ΔWi​)2. The theorem is that Qn→tQ_n \to tQn​→t in L2L^2L2; one writes [W]t=t[W]_t = t[W]t​=t. The proof is short. Since ΔWi∼N(0,Δti)\Delta W_i \sim N(0, \Delta t_i)ΔWi​∼N(0,Δti​) independently, and Var⁡(Z2)=2σ4\operatorname{Var}(Z^2) = 2\sigma^4Var(Z2)=2σ4 for Z∼N(0,σ2)Z \sim N(0, \sigma^2)Z∼N(0,σ2),

E[Qn]=∑iΔti=t,Var⁡(Qn)=∑i2 Δti2≤2t δn⟶0.\mathbb{E}[Q_n] = \sum_i \Delta t_i = t, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(Q_n) = \sum_i 2\,\Delta t_i^2 \le 2t\,\delta_n \longrightarrow 0.E[Qn​]=i∑​Δti​=t,Var(Qn​)=i∑​2Δti2​≤2tδn​⟶0.

So E[(Qn−t)2]→0\mathbb{E}[(Q_n - t)^2] \to 0E[(Qn​−t)2]→0. (Along dyadic partitions the convergence is also almost sure.) The total variation is then infinite: Qn≤max⁡i∣ΔWi∣⋅∑i∣ΔWi∣Q_n \le \max_i |\Delta W_i| \cdot \sum_i |\Delta W_i|Qn​≤maxi​∣ΔWi​∣⋅∑i​∣ΔWi​∣, and max⁡i∣ΔWi∣→0\max_i |\Delta W_i| \to 0maxi​∣ΔWi​∣→0 by uniform continuity of the path on [0,t][0, t][0,t], so ∑i∣ΔWi∣→∞\sum_i |\Delta W_i| \to \infty∑i​∣ΔWi​∣→∞ along a subsequence on which Qn→tQ_n \to tQn​→t almost surely. A smooth function has finite total variation and zero quadratic variation; Brownian motion has the opposite. This is why ∫H dW\int H\,dW∫HdW cannot be defined path by path as an ordinary Stieltjes integral.

Markov and strong Markov property

The time-shift invariance states the Markov property: for every fixed s≥0s \ge 0s≥0, the process (Ws+t−Ws)t≥0(W_{s+t} - W_s)_{t \ge 0}(Ws+t​−Ws​)t≥0​ is a Brownian motion independent of Fs\mathcal{F}_sFs​. Equivalently, for bounded measurable fff, E[f(Ws+t)∣Fs]=g(Ws)\mathbb{E}[f(W_{s+t}) \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = g(W_s)E[f(Ws+t​)∣Fs​]=g(Ws​) with g(x)=E[f(x+Wt)]g(x) = \mathbb{E}[f(x + W_t)]g(x)=E[f(x+Wt​)]: the forecast depends on the past only through the present value.

The strong Markov property (Hunt, 1956) allows the fixed time sss to be replaced by a stopping time τ\tauτ: on {τ<∞}\{\tau < \infty\}{τ<∞}, the process (Wτ+t−Wτ)t≥0(W_{\tau+t} - W_\tau)_{t \ge 0}(Wτ+t​−Wτ​)t≥0​ is a Brownian motion independent of Fτ\mathcal{F}_\tauFτ​. Think of it as a restart rule: whenever you stop using only the information available so far, what happens next is a fresh Brownian motion. A random time that looks into the future, such as the time of the maximum on [0,1][0, 1][0,1], does not have this property. The next lesson uses the strong Markov property at the first time the path reaches a level.

Summary

  • Brownian motion: W0=0W_0 = 0W0​=0, independent increments, Wt−Ws∼N(0,t−s)W_t - W_s \sim N(0, t-s)Wt​−Ws​∼N(0,t−s), continuous paths; equivalently, a continuous centred Gaussian process with Cov⁡(Ws,Wt)=min⁡(s,t)\operatorname{Cov}(W_s, W_t) = \min(s,t)Cov(Ws​,Wt​)=min(s,t).
  • Symmetry −Wt-W_t−Wt​, scaling c−1/2Wctc^{-1/2}W_{ct}c−1/2Wct​, time shift Ws+t−WsW_{s+t} - W_sWs+t​−Ws​ and time inversion t W1/tt\,W_{1/t}tW1/t​ are again Brownian motions; time inversion gives Wt/t→0W_t/t \to 0Wt​/t→0.
  • Paths are recurrent, obey the law of the iterated logarithm and are nowhere differentiable.
  • Quadratic variation [W]t=t[W]_t = t[W]t​=t, total variation infinite.
  • Markov property at fixed times, strong Markov property at stopping times.
