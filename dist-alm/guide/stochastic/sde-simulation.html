Simulating SDEs: the Euler–Maruyama scheme

Lesson 14 of 17 · 1 min read

In "Monte Carlo option pricing" every terminal price was drawn in one step, because "Itô's lemma" gave the exact solution of geometric Brownian motion. Most models used in practice have no such formula. This lesson shows how to simulate a stochastic differential equation step by step with the Euler–Maruyama scheme, how to measure the error it makes, and how fast that error shrinks as the steps get smaller. Geometric Brownian motion serves as the test case, precisely because its exact solution is known.

Stochastic differential equations

A stochastic differential equation (SDE) describes how a quantity XtX_tXt​ changes over a short time dtdtdt:

dXt=a(Xt,t) dt+b(Xt,t) dWt.dX_t = a(X_t, t)\,dt + b(X_t, t)\,dW_t.dXt​=a(Xt​,t)dt+b(Xt​,t)dWt​.

The drift aaa is the expected rate of change, and the diffusion coefficient bbb scales the random shock dWtdW_tdWt​ of a Brownian motion. Geometric Brownian motion is the case a=μXa = \mu Xa=μX, b=σXb = \sigma Xb=σX. A mean-reverting interest rate, or the variance in "Stochastic volatility and the Heston model", has other coefficients, and for many of them no closed-form solution exists.

The Euler–Maruyama step

Split [0,T][0, T][0,T] into nnn steps of length Δt=T/n\Delta t = T/nΔt=T/n and freeze the coefficients at the start of each step. The Brownian increment over a step is normal with variance Δt\Delta tΔt, so it can be written ΔWk=Δt Zk\Delta W_k = \sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z_kΔWk​=Δt​Zk​ with ZkZ_kZk​ standard normal. The Euler–Maruyama scheme is

Xk+1=Xk+a(Xk,tk) Δt+b(Xk,tk) Δt Zk.X_{k+1} = X_k + a(X_k, t_k)\,\Delta t + b(X_k, t_k)\,\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z_k.Xk+1​=Xk​+a(Xk​,tk​)Δt+b(Xk​,tk​)Δt​Zk​.

It is the natural first approximation: it keeps exactly the two terms that dominate the Itô expansion of the change over one step, the dtdtdt term and the dWdWdW term, and drops everything smaller. Think of it as walking along a curved road by taking straight steps in the direction you are facing; the shorter the steps, the closer you stay to the road.

For geometric Brownian motion one step reads Sk+1=Sk (1+μ Δt+σ ΔWk)S_{k+1} = S_k\,(1 + \mu\,\Delta t + \sigma\,\Delta W_k)Sk+1​=Sk​(1+μΔt+σΔWk​), while the exact solution over the same step is

Sk+1=Skexp⁡ ⁣((μ−12σ2)Δt+σ ΔWk).S_{k+1} = S_k \exp\!\Big(\big(\mu - \tfrac12\sigma^2\big)\Delta t + \sigma\,\Delta W_k\Big).Sk+1​=Sk​exp((μ−21​σ2)Δt+σΔWk​).

Strong and weak error

There are two ways to judge a simulation scheme, depending on what it is used for.

  • The strong error E[ ∣XT−X^T∣ ]\mathbb{E}\big[\,|X_T - \hat X_T|\,\big]E[∣XT​−X^T​∣] compares the simulated path X^\hat XX^ with the true path driven by the same Brownian motion. It matters when the path itself matters, for example when hedging along it.
  • The weak error ∣ E[f(XT)]−E[f(X^T)] ∣\big|\,\mathbb{E}[f(X_T)] - \mathbb{E}[f(\hat X_T)]\,\big|​E[f(XT​)]−E[f(X^T​)]​ compares only averages. It is what matters for Monte Carlo pricing, which needs the distribution of XTX_TXT​, not individual paths.

For smooth coefficients the Euler–Maruyama scheme has strong order 12\tfrac1221​ and weak order 1: the strong error shrinks like Δt\sqrt{\Delta t}Δt​, the weak error like Δt\Delta tΔt. Four times as many steps halve the strong error but cut the weak error to a quarter.

Geometric Brownian motion makes the weak error easy to compute. Each Euler step multiplies the expected value by 1+μ Δt1 + \mu\,\Delta t1+μΔt, so E[S^T]=S0 (1+μT/n)n\mathbb{E}[\hat S_T] = S_0\,(1 + \mu T/n)^nE[S^T​]=S0​(1+μT/n)n, against the exact S0 eμTS_0\,e^{\mu T}S0​eμT. With the numbers above, 100 e0.08=108.3287100\,e^{0.08} = 108.3287100e0.08=108.3287, and the gaps for n=4n = 4n=4, 161616 and 646464 are 0.08550.08550.0855, 0.02160.02160.0216 and 0.00540.00540.0054: a quarter each time.

Simulating the logarithm instead

For geometric Brownian motion there is a better option than discretising SSS itself. By "Itô's lemma", ln⁡St\ln S_tlnSt​ follows

d(ln⁡St)=(μ−12σ2) dt+σ dWt,d(\ln S_t) = \big(\mu - \tfrac12\sigma^2\big)\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t,d(lnSt​)=(μ−21​σ2)dt+σdWt​,

whose coefficients are constant. Euler steps on ln⁡S\ln SlnS are therefore exact, whatever Δt\Delta tΔt is, and prices stay positive automatically. Euler on SSS can even produce a negative price if 1+μ Δt+σ ΔW1 + \mu\,\Delta t + \sigma\,\Delta W1+μΔt+σΔW falls below zero. Transforming a model into variables with simpler coefficients before discretising is a standard trick; when no such transformation exists, as for many mean-reverting or stochastic-volatility models, small steps and the error orders above are what you rely on.

Summary

  • An SDE dX=a dt+b dWdX = a\,dt + b\,dWdX=adt+bdW is simulated step by step with Euler–Maruyama: Xk+1=Xk+a Δt+bΔt ZkX_{k+1} = X_k + a\,\Delta t + b\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z_kXk+1​=Xk​+aΔt+bΔt​Zk​.
  • The strong error (paths) has order 12\tfrac1221​ and the weak error (averages) order 1; Monte Carlo pricing only needs the weak one.
  • Coarse and fine grids must use the same Brownian path to be compared: coarse increments are sums of fine ones.
  • When a transformation gives constant coefficients, as ln⁡S\ln SlnS does for geometric Brownian motion, simulate that variable instead: the steps become exact.

A later lesson, "Mean reversion: Ornstein–Uhlenbeck and Vasicek", studies a process whose drift pulls it back towards a long-run level, and simulates it with exactly this kind of step.
