Mean reversion: Ornstein–Uhlenbeck and Vasicek

Lesson 16 of 17 · 1 min read

Brownian motion from "Random walks and Brownian motion" and the stock price model built on it wander without limit: their variance grows with the horizon, and nothing pulls them back. Interest rates, volatility and credit spreads behave differently. They fluctuate, but over years they tend to return to a typical level. This lesson builds the standard model of such a process, solves it exactly with the tools of "Itô's lemma", and applies it to the short interest rate in the Vasicek model.

The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process

Let XtX_tXt​ be the quantity of interest, for example an interest rate. The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process is the Itô process

dXt=κ (θ−Xt) dt+σ dWt,dX_t = \kappa\,(\theta - X_t)\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t,dXt​=κ(θ−Xt​)dt+σdWt​,

with three constant parameters:

  • θ\thetaθ is the long-run level, the value the process is pulled towards.
  • κ>0\kappa > 0κ>0 is the speed of mean reversion: how strongly the gap θ−Xt\theta - X_tθ−Xt​ is closed per unit of time.
  • σ>0\sigma > 0σ>0 is the volatility: the size of the random shocks, as in "Itô's lemma".

The drift has the sign of the gap. Above θ\thetaθ the drift is negative and pushes the process down; below θ\thetaθ it is positive and pushes it up. Think of a ball attached to a spring in a stream of random kicks: the further it is pulled from its rest point, the harder the spring pulls back. Unlike geometric Brownian motion, the volatility here does not depend on the level, so the shocks have the same size everywhere.

The deterministic part

Switch off the noise, σ=0\sigma = 0σ=0. The equation becomes the ordinary differential equation dx/dt=κ(θ−x)dx/dt = \kappa(\theta - x)dx/dt=κ(θ−x), whose solution is

x(t)=θ+(x0−θ) e−κt.x(t) = \theta + (x_0 - \theta)\,e^{-\kappa t}.x(t)=θ+(x0​−θ)e−κt.

The gap to θ\thetaθ shrinks exponentially. The time after which half of the gap is left solves e−κt=1/2e^{-\kappa t} = 1/2e−κt=1/2 and is called the half-life:

t1/2=ln⁡2κ.t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\kappa}.t1/2​=κln2​.

It does not depend on how large the gap is. With κ=0.5\kappa = 0.5κ=0.5 the half-life is ln⁡2/0.5≈1.39\ln 2 / 0.5 \approx 1.39ln2/0.5≈1.39 years; with κ=5\kappa = 5κ=5 it is about 35 trading days.

Exact solution

The trick is to remove the pull towards θ\thetaθ by a change of variable. Apply Itô's lemma to Yt=f(t,Xt)=eκtXtY_t = f(t, X_t) = e^{\kappa t} X_tYt​=f(t,Xt​)=eκtXt​, with ∂f/∂t=κeκtx\partial f/\partial t = \kappa e^{\kappa t} x∂f/∂t=κeκtx, ∂f/∂x=eκt\partial f/\partial x = e^{\kappa t}∂f/∂x=eκt and ∂2f/∂x2=0\partial^2 f/\partial x^2 = 0∂2f/∂x2=0. Since the second derivative is zero, there is no Itô correction:

dYt=κeκtXt dt+eκt(κ(θ−Xt) dt+σ dWt)=κθ eκt dt+σeκt dWt.dY_t = \kappa e^{\kappa t} X_t\,dt + e^{\kappa t}\big(\kappa(\theta - X_t)\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t\big) = \kappa\theta\,e^{\kappa t}\,dt + \sigma e^{\kappa t}\,dW_t.dYt​=κeκtXt​dt+eκt(κ(θ−Xt​)dt+σdWt​)=κθeκtdt+σeκtdWt​.

The terms with XtX_tXt​ cancel, and the right-hand side no longer depends on YYY. Add up the increments over [0,t][0, t][0,t]:

eκtXt=X0+θ (eκt−1)+σ∫0teκs dWs.e^{\kappa t} X_t = X_0 + \theta\,\big(e^{\kappa t} - 1\big) + \sigma \int_0^t e^{\kappa s}\,dW_s.eκtXt​=X0​+θ(eκt−1)+σ∫0t​eκsdWs​.

Multiplying by e−κte^{-\kappa t}e−κt gives the solution:

Xt=θ+(X0−θ) e−κt+σ∫0te−κ(t−s) dWs.X_t = \theta + (X_0 - \theta)\,e^{-\kappa t} + \sigma \int_0^t e^{-\kappa (t-s)}\,dW_s.Xt​=θ+(X0​−θ)e−κt+σ∫0t​e−κ(t−s)dWs​.

The first two terms are the deterministic path from above. The integral adds up the shocks, each weighted by e−κ(t−s)e^{-\kappa(t-s)}e−κ(t−s): a shock that happened long ago has mostly been pulled back and counts little.

Mean, variance and stationary distribution

The integral of a deterministic function against dWdWdW is normal with mean zero, and by the Itô isometry its variance is the integral of the squared weight: Var⁡(∫0tg(s) dWs)=∫0tg(s)2 ds\operatorname{Var}\big(\int_0^t g(s)\,dW_s\big) = \int_0^t g(s)^2\,dsVar(∫0t​g(s)dWs​)=∫0t​g(s)2ds. With g(s)=σe−κ(t−s)g(s) = \sigma e^{-\kappa(t-s)}g(s)=σe−κ(t−s) this gives

E[Xt]=θ+(X0−θ) e−κt,Var⁡(Xt)=σ22κ(1−e−2κt).\mathbb{E}[X_t] = \theta + (X_0 - \theta)\,e^{-\kappa t}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X_t) = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa}\big(1 - e^{-2\kappa t}\big).E[Xt​]=θ+(X0​−θ)e−κt,Var(Xt​)=2κσ2​(1−e−2κt).

So XtX_tXt​ is normally distributed for every ttt. As t→∞t \to \inftyt→∞ the mean tends to θ\thetaθ and the variance stops growing. Compare Brownian motion, whose variance σ2t\sigma^2 tσ2t grows forever.

Stationary distribution of the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process: X∞∼N(θ, σ2/(2κ))X_\infty \sim N\big(\theta,\ \sigma^2/(2\kappa)\big)X∞​∼N(θ, σ2/(2κ)). Faster mean reversion (larger κ\kappaκ) means a narrower long-run spread around θ\thetaθ.

Exact simulation and the AR(1) form

The same calculation over one step of length Δt\Delta tΔt, started from the current value, gives an exact simulation scheme with no discretisation error. With Z∼N(0,1)Z \sim N(0,1)Z∼N(0,1) drawn independently at each step:

Xt+Δt=θ+(Xt−θ) e−κΔt+σ1−e−2κΔt2κ  Z.X_{t+\Delta t} = \theta + (X_t - \theta)\,e^{-\kappa\Delta t} + \sigma\sqrt{\frac{1 - e^{-2\kappa\Delta t}}{2\kappa}}\;Z.Xt+Δt​=θ+(Xt​−θ)e−κΔt+σ2κ1−e−2κΔt​​Z.

Sampled at a fixed step, the process is a first-order autoregression, AR(1): Xn+1=a+ϕXn+εn+1X_{n+1} = a + \phi X_n + \varepsilon_{n+1}Xn+1​=a+ϕXn​+εn+1​ with ϕ=e−κΔt\phi = e^{-\kappa\Delta t}ϕ=e−κΔt and a=θ(1−ϕ)a = \theta(1 - \phi)a=θ(1−ϕ). Regressing observed values on their previous values therefore estimates the parameters: κ=−ln⁡ϕ/Δt\kappa = -\ln\phi / \Delta tκ=−lnϕ/Δt and θ=a/(1−ϕ)\theta = a/(1-\phi)θ=a/(1−ϕ).

Take κ=0.5\kappa = 0.5κ=0.5, θ=4%\theta = 4\%θ=4%, σ=1%\sigma = 1\%σ=1% and a starting level X0=8%X_0 = 8\%X0​=8%, with time in years.

  • Half-life: ln⁡2/0.5≈1.39\ln 2 / 0.5 \approx 1.39ln2/0.5≈1.39 years.
  • After 1 year: mean 4%+4%⋅e−0.5≈6.43%4\% + 4\% \cdot e^{-0.5} \approx 6.43\%4%+4%⋅e−0.5≈6.43%, standard deviation 0.01(1−e−1)/1≈0.80%0.01\sqrt{(1 - e^{-1})/1} \approx 0.80\%0.01(1−e−1)/1​≈0.80%.

The Vasicek model

The short rate rtr_trt​ is the continuously compounded rate for borrowing over an instant. The Vasicek model assumes that it follows an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, drt=κ(θ−rt) dt+σ dWtdr_t = \kappa(\theta - r_t)\,dt + \sigma\,dW_tdrt​=κ(θ−rt​)dt+σdWt​, under the risk-neutral measure Q\mathbb{Q}Q. A zero-coupon bond pays 1 at time TTT, and its price is the expected discount factor along the random path of the short rate, P(0,T)=EQ[e−∫0Trs ds]P(0,T) = \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\big[e^{-\int_0^T r_s\,ds}\big]P(0,T)=EQ[e−∫0T​rs​ds]. Because the integral of a normal process is normal, this expectation has a closed form:

P(0,T)=A(T) e−B(T) r0,B(T)=1−e−κTκ,P(0,T) = A(T)\,e^{-B(T)\,r_0}, \qquad B(T) = \frac{1 - e^{-\kappa T}}{\kappa},P(0,T)=A(T)e−B(T)r0​,B(T)=κ1−e−κT​,
ln⁡A(T)=(θ−σ22κ2)(B(T)−T)−σ2B(T)24κ.\ln A(T) = \left(\theta - \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa^2}\right)\big(B(T) - T\big) - \frac{\sigma^2 B(T)^2}{4\kappa}.lnA(T)=(θ−2κ2σ2​)(B(T)−T)−4κσ2B(T)2​.

The yield lies between r0r_0r0​ and θ\thetaθ, since the market expects the rate to fall back towards 4%.

As TTT grows the yield tends to θ−σ2/(2κ2)\theta - \sigma^2/(2\kappa^2)θ−σ2/(2κ2), here 3.98%3.98\%3.98%. If r0r_0r0​ is below this level the model produces a rising curve, if above an inverted (falling) curve, and for r0r_0r0​ just above it a slightly humped one that rises briefly before falling. The lesson "Spot rates, forward rates and the yield curve" describes these shapes as observed in the market; the Vasicek model can produce only these few, which is why practitioners often fit richer curves directly. The main drawback is that a normal short rate can become negative with positive probability. The Cox–Ingersoll–Ross (CIR) model avoids this by making the volatility proportional to rt\sqrt{r_t}rt​​.

  • The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process dX=κ(θ−X) dt+σ dWdX = \kappa(\theta - X)\,dt + \sigma\,dWdX=κ(θ−X)dt+σdW is pulled towards the long-run level θ\thetaθ with speed κ\kappaκ; the expected gap halves every ln⁡2/κ\ln 2/\kappaln2/κ.
  • Itô's lemma applied to eκtXte^{\kappa t}X_teκtXt​ gives the exact solution; XtX_tXt​ is normal with mean θ+(X0−θ)e−κt\theta + (X_0 - \theta)e^{-\kappa t}θ+(X0​−θ)e−κt and variance σ22κ(1−e−2κt)\tfrac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa}(1 - e^{-2\kappa t})2κσ2​(1−e−2κt).
