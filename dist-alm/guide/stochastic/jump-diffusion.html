Jumps and the Merton jump-diffusion model

Lesson 17 of 17 · 1 min read

Every model in this track so far moves continuously: Brownian motion from "Random walks and Brownian motion", "Geometric Brownian motion" built on it, and the processes of "Mean reversion: Ornstein–Uhlenbeck and Vasicek". Real prices sometimes gap. An earnings surprise, a default or a market crash moves the price by several percent between two trades, with nothing in between. This lesson adds such jumps to geometric Brownian motion and works out their effect on returns, option prices and hedging.

The Poisson process

Jumps arrive at random times, so we first need a model for counting them. The Poisson process NtN_tNt​ counts the events up to time ttt. It starts at N0=0N_0 = 0N0​=0, increases by exactly 1 at each event and is constant in between. Its single parameter is the intensity λ>0\lambda > 0λ>0, the expected number of events per year. The modelling assumptions are:

  • In a short interval of length dtdtdt an event occurs with probability λ dt\lambda\,dtλdt, and two or more events with negligible probability.
  • The numbers of events in non-overlapping intervals are independent (independent increments), as for Brownian motion.

From these assumptions the number of events up to ttt follows the Poisson distribution:

Pr⁡(Nt=n)=e−λt (λt)nn!,n=0,1,2,…\Pr(N_t = n) = e^{-\lambda t}\,\frac{(\lambda t)^n}{n!}, \qquad n = 0, 1, 2, \ldotsPr(Nt​=n)=e−λtn!(λt)n​,n=0,1,2,…

Its mean and its variance are both λt\lambda tλt.

The compound Poisson process

A count alone does not move a price; each event needs a size. Let J1,J2,…J_1, J_2, \ldotsJ1​,J2​,… be independent, identically distributed jump sizes, independent of NNN. The compound Poisson process adds up the jumps that have occurred so far:

Yt=∑i=1NtJi.Y_t = \sum_{i=1}^{N_t} J_i.Yt​=i=1∑Nt​​Ji​.

Think of the claims arriving at an insurer: NtN_tNt​ is the number of claims, JiJ_iJi​ the size of each, and YtY_tYt​ the total paid. Conditioning on the number of jumps gives the mean E[Yt]=λt E[J]\mathbb{E}[Y_t] = \lambda t\,\mathbb{E}[J]E[Yt​]=λtE[J] and the variance Var⁡(Yt)=λt E[J2]\operatorname{Var}(Y_t) = \lambda t\,\mathbb{E}[J^2]Var(Yt​)=λtE[J2].

The Merton jump-diffusion model

Merton (1976) adds such jumps to geometric Brownian motion. The jumps act on the log price: when a jump of size JJJ occurs, the price is multiplied by eJe^JeJ, so its relative change is eJ−1e^J - 1eJ−1. Merton assumes normal log-jumps, J∼N(μJ,σJ2)J \sim N(\mu_J, \sigma_J^2)J∼N(μJ​,σJ2​), independent of each other, of NNN and of WWW. The dynamics are

dStSt−=(μ−λκ) dt+σ dWt+(eJ−1) dNt,κ=E[eJ−1]=eμJ+σJ2/2−1.\frac{dS_t}{S_{t-}} = (\mu - \lambda\kappa)\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t + \big(e^{J} - 1\big)\,dN_t, \qquad \kappa = \mathbb{E}\big[e^{J} - 1\big] = e^{\mu_J + \sigma_J^2/2} - 1.St−​dSt​​=(μ−λκ)dt+σdWt​+(eJ−1)dNt​,κ=E[eJ−1]=eμJ​+σJ2​/2−1.

St−S_{t-}St−​ is the price just before a possible jump. Between jumps the price follows geometric Brownian motion; at a jump it is multiplied by eJe^JeJ. The jump term has expected value λκ dt\lambda\kappa\,dtλκdt per instant, so the compensator −λκ-\lambda\kappa−λκ in the drift cancels it and the expected return stays μ\muμ. With μJ=−10%\mu_J = -10\%μJ​=−10% and σJ=15%\sigma_J = 15\%σJ​=15%, κ≈−8.5%\kappa \approx -8.5\%κ≈−8.5%: an average jump costs 8.5% of the price, and with λ=0.5\lambda = 0.5λ=0.5 the compensator raises the drift by about 4.25% per year.

Under the risk-neutral measure Q\mathbb{Q}Q the discounted price must be a martingale, so μ\muμ is replaced by rrr and the drift becomes r−λκr - \lambda\kappar−λκ. "Change of measure and Girsanov's theorem" covered only the Brownian part, where a change of measure shifts the drift of WWW. With jumps the compensator term −λκ-\lambda\kappa−λκ is needed as well, otherwise the expected jump loss would make the discounted price drift.

Applying "Itô's lemma" to ln⁡S\ln SlnS between jumps gives the familiar −σ2/2-\sigma^2/2−σ2/2 correction, and each jump adds JJJ to the log price. The log return over [0,T][0, T][0,T] is therefore

ln⁡STS0=(μ−λκ−σ22)T+σWT+∑i=1NTJi.\ln\frac{S_T}{S_0} = \Big(\mu - \lambda\kappa - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\Big)T + \sigma W_T + \sum_{i=1}^{N_T} J_i.lnS0​ST​​=(μ−λκ−2σ2​)T+σWT​+i=1∑NT​​Ji​.

The distribution of the log return

Condition on the number of jumps. Given NT=nN_T = nNT​=n, the log return is a constant plus σWT\sigma W_TσWT​ plus nnn independent normal jumps, a sum of independent normals. Writing m=(μ−λκ−σ2/2)Tm = (\mu - \lambda\kappa - \sigma^2/2)Tm=(μ−λκ−σ2/2)T,

ln⁡STS0  ∣  NT=n  ∼  N(m+nμJ, σ2T+nσJ2).\ln\frac{S_T}{S_0}\;\Big|\;N_T = n \;\sim\; N\big(m + n\mu_J,\ \sigma^2 T + n\sigma_J^2\big).lnS0​ST​​​NT​=n∼N(m+nμJ​, σ2T+nσJ2​).

Averaging over nnn with the Poisson weights gives the density fff of the log return as a Poisson mixture of normals:

f(x)=∑n=0∞e−λT(λT)nn!  φ(x; m+nμJ, σ2T+nσJ2),f(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} e^{-\lambda T}\frac{(\lambda T)^n}{n!}\;\varphi\big(x;\ m + n\mu_J,\ \sigma^2 T + n\sigma_J^2\big),f(x)=n=0∑∞​e−λTn!(λT)n​φ(x; m+nμJ​, σ2T+nσJ2​),

where φ(x;a,b)\varphi(x; a, b)φ(x;a,b) is the normal density with mean aaa and variance bbb. Each normal component is a scenario with a given number of jumps.

Moments and fat tails

The shape is easiest to read from cumulants. The first two are the mean and the variance; the third and fourth, divided by the matching powers of the standard deviation, give the skewness and the excess kurtosis (kurtosis minus 3, zero for a normal). Cumulants of independent sums add. The diffusion part is normal and contributes only to the variance, σ2T\sigma^2 Tσ2T. For a compound Poisson sum the kkk-th cumulant is λT E[Jk]\lambda T\,\mathbb{E}[J^k]λTE[Jk]. Hence

Var⁡=σ2T+λT(μJ2+σJ2),c3=λT(μJ3+3μJσJ2),c4=λT(μJ4+6μJ2σJ2+3σJ4),\operatorname{Var} = \sigma^2 T + \lambda T(\mu_J^2 + \sigma_J^2), \qquad c_3 = \lambda T(\mu_J^3 + 3\mu_J\sigma_J^2), \qquad c_4 = \lambda T(\mu_J^4 + 6\mu_J^2\sigma_J^2 + 3\sigma_J^4),Var=σ2T+λT(μJ2​+σJ2​),c3​=λT(μJ3​+3μJ​σJ2​),c4​=λT(μJ4​+6μJ2​σJ2​+3σJ4​),

with skewness c3/Var⁡3/2c_3/\operatorname{Var}^{3/2}c3​/Var3/2 and excess kurtosis c4/Var⁡2c_4/\operatorname{Var}^2c4​/Var2. When μJ<0\mu_J < 0μJ​<0, c3c_3c3​ is negative: the distribution has a longer left tail. c4c_4c4​ is always positive, so the tails are fat: extreme returns are more likely than under a normal with the same variance. Both effects shrink with the horizon, since skewness scales like 1/T1/\sqrt{T}1/T​ and excess kurtosis like 1/T1/T1/T.

In the Merton model the log return is a Poisson mixture of normals. Its variance is σ2T+λT(μJ2+σJ2)\sigma^2T + \lambda T(\mu_J^2 + \sigma_J^2)σ2T+λT(μJ2​+σJ2​); negative mean jumps give negative skew, and the excess kurtosis is always positive.

Option prices and the smile

Merton assumed that jump risk is specific to the firm and can be diversified away, so the jump intensity and the jump distribution are the same under Q\mathbb{Q}Q as under P\mathbb{P}P. Conditional on nnn jumps the price at TTT is log-normal, so the conditional option price is a Black–Scholes price. Averaging over nnn gives Merton's formula:

C0=∑n=0∞e−λ′T(λ′T)nn!  CBS(S0,K,T,σn,rn),C_0 = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} e^{-\lambda' T}\frac{(\lambda' T)^n}{n!}\;C^{\text{BS}}\big(S_0, K, T, \sigma_n, r_n\big),C0​=n=0∑∞​e−λ′Tn!(λ′T)n​CBS(S0​,K,T,σn​,rn​),
λ′=λ(1+κ),σn2=σ2+nσJ2T,rn=r−λκ+nln⁡(1+κ)T.\lambda' = \lambda(1 + \kappa), \qquad \sigma_n^2 = \sigma^2 + \frac{n\sigma_J^2}{T}, \qquad r_n = r - \lambda\kappa + \frac{n\ln(1 + \kappa)}{T}.λ′=λ(1+κ),σn2​=σ2+TnσJ2​​,rn​=r−λκ+Tnln(1+κ)​.

Each term is the Black–Scholes price of the scenario with nnn jumps.

Invert these prices to implied volatilities, as in "Implied volatility and the volatility smile". Because the risk-neutral distribution has a fat, negatively skewed left tail, out-of-the-money puts are worth more than Black–Scholes with a single volatility allows, and the implied volatility rises for low strikes: a skew. Since skewness and excess kurtosis decay with the horizon, the skew is steepest at short maturities and flattens for long ones. A pure diffusion behaves differently: over a very short horizon its log return is almost normal, so it struggles to produce a steep short-dated skew. Jumps are one way to explain the short-dated skew seen in equity markets; the other, a random volatility, is the subject of "Stochastic volatility and the Heston model", and practical models often combine both. The Heston lab lets you price options under stochastic volatility for comparison.

Hedging and incompleteness

In the Black–Scholes setting a position of Δ\DeltaΔ shares, rebalanced continuously, removes all risk from an option. With jumps this fails. When a jump occurs, the option changes by C(SeJ)−C(S)C(S e^J) - C(S)C(SeJ)−C(S), which is not linear in the random jump size eJ−1e^J - 1eJ−1. A stock position can offset the change for one jump size but not for all of them, and rebalancing cannot react to an instantaneous jump. Some risk remains, and the market with only the stock and the bond is incomplete: the option price is no longer fixed by replication but depends on how the market prices jump risk.

Summary

  • The Poisson process NtN_tNt​ counts events with intensity λ\lambdaλ: Pr⁡(Nt=n)=e−λt(λt)n/n!\Pr(N_t = n) = e^{-\lambda t}(\lambda t)^n/n!Pr(Nt​=n)=e−λt(λt)n/n!, mean and variance λt\lambda tλt, independent increments.
  • The Merton model dS/S−=(μ−λκ) dt+σ dW+(eJ−1) dNdS/S_{-} = (\mu - \lambda\kappa)\,dt + \sigma\,dW + (e^J - 1)\,dNdS/S−​=(μ−λκ)dt+σdW+(eJ−1)dN adds normal log-jumps to geometric Brownian motion; the compensator −λκ-\lambda\kappa−λκ keeps the expected return, and under Q\mathbb{Q}Q makes the discounted price a martingale.
  • The log return is a Poisson mixture of normals with variance σ2T+λT(μJ2+σJ2)\sigma^2T + \lambda T(\mu_J^2 + \sigma_J^2)σ2T+λT(μJ2​+σJ2​), negative skew for μJ<0\mu_J < 0μJ​<0 and fat tails, strongest at short horizons.
  • Merton's option price is a Poisson-weighted sum of Black–Scholes prices; the implied volatility shows a skew that is steepest at short maturities.
  • A jump cannot be delta-hedged, so the market is incomplete.
