Change of measure and Girsanov's theorem

Lesson 12 of 17 · 1 min read

Option pricing works with two sets of probabilities for the same outcomes: the real-world measure P\mathbb{P}P and the risk-neutral measure Q\mathbb{Q}Q. The lesson "Risk-neutral probabilities" introduced them on a one-step tree. This lesson shows how one measure is turned into the other by reweighting outcomes, first on the tree and then for Brownian motion, using the tools from "Random walks and Brownian motion" and "Itô's lemma". The continuous-time result is Girsanov's theorem.

Two measures on the same outcomes

A probability measure assigns a probability to every event. Two measures can share the same set of possible outcomes and disagree only about how likely each one is. In the one-step binomial model the stock goes up or down; under P\mathbb{P}P the up move has probability ppp, under Q\mathbb{Q}Q it has probability qqq. The outcomes, and hence every payoff, are identical. Only the weights differ.

A fair picture is a die that is reloaded: the six faces stay the same, but the chance of each face changes. Changing the measure means reloading the die, not repainting it.

The density between two measures

Define a random variable ZZZ that, in each outcome, is the ratio of the two probabilities. On the tree it takes two values:

Zu=qp,Zd=1−q1−p.Z_u = \frac{q}{p}, \qquad Z_d = \frac{1-q}{1-p}.Zu​=pq​,Zd​=1−p1−q​.

Multiplying by ZZZ converts a P\mathbb{P}P-expectation into a Q\mathbb{Q}Q-expectation. For any payoff XXX with values XuX_uXu​ and XdX_dXd​:

EP[ZX]=p qp Xu+(1−p) 1−q1−p Xd=qXu+(1−q)Xd=EQ[X].\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[Z X] = p\,\frac{q}{p}\,X_u + (1-p)\,\frac{1-q}{1-p}\,X_d = q X_u + (1-q) X_d = \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[X].EP[ZX]=ppq​Xu​+(1−p)1−p1−q​Xd​=qXu​+(1−q)Xd​=EQ[X].

Taking X=1X = 1X=1 shows that EP[Z]=1\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[Z] = 1EP[Z]=1, and ZZZ is positive in every outcome. In general such a ZZZ is called the Radon–Nikodym derivative and is written Z=dQ/dPZ = d\mathbb{Q}/d\mathbb{P}Z=dQ/dP.

Example: with S0=100S_0 = 100S0​=100, u=1.2u = 1.2u=1.2, d=0.9d = 0.9d=0.9 and r=2%r = 2\%r=2%, the risk-neutral probability is q=0.4q = 0.4q=0.4. Suppose the real probability is p=0.6p = 0.6p=0.6. Then Zu=0.4/0.6≈0.667Z_u = 0.4/0.6 \approx 0.667Zu​=0.4/0.6≈0.667 and Zd=0.6/0.4=1.5Z_d = 0.6/0.4 = 1.5Zd​=0.6/0.4=1.5. The check 0.6⋅0.667+0.4⋅1.5=10.6 \cdot 0.667 + 0.4 \cdot 1.5 = 10.6⋅0.667+0.4⋅1.5=1 holds. For a call with strike 100, Xu=20X_u = 20Xu​=20 and Xd=0X_d = 0Xd​=0, so EP[ZX]=0.6⋅0.667⋅20=8\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[ZX] = 0.6 \cdot 0.667 \cdot 20 = 8EP[ZX]=0.6⋅0.667⋅20=8, which equals EQ[X]=0.4⋅20=8\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[X] = 0.4 \cdot 20 = 8EQ[X]=0.4⋅20=8. Discounting gives the call price 8/1.02≈7.848/1.02 \approx 7.848/1.02≈7.84. The density shrinks the weight of the good outcome and raises the weight of the bad one.

Equivalent measures

The density only makes sense if ppp and 1−p1 - p1−p are both positive, so that nothing is divided by zero. Two measures are equivalent when they agree on which events are impossible: an event has probability zero under P\mathbb{P}P exactly when it has probability zero under Q\mathbb{Q}Q. Then ZZZ is strictly positive and the change of measure can be undone with 1/Z1/Z1/Z.

This is the condition that matters for pricing. An arbitrage is a strategy that cannot lose and may win. Whether something is possible or impossible is the same under equivalent measures, so a market free of arbitrage under P\mathbb{P}P is free of arbitrage under Q\mathbb{Q}Q as well.

Two measures are equivalent if they have the same impossible events. Between equivalent measures, EQ[X]=EP[ZX]\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[X] = \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[ZX]EQ[X]=EP[ZX] with a strictly positive density Z=dQ/dPZ = d\mathbb{Q}/d\mathbb{P}Z=dQ/dP that has EP[Z]=1\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[Z] = 1EP[Z]=1.

Shifting the mean of a normal variable

Now let the outcome be continuous. Under P\mathbb{P}P let WT∼N(0,T)W_T \sim N(0, T)WT​∼N(0,T), the value of a Brownian motion at time TTT. Fix a number θ\thetaθ and define

Z=exp⁡ ⁣(−θWT−12θ2T).Z = \exp\!\left(-\theta W_T - \tfrac{1}{2}\theta^2 T\right).Z=exp(−θWT​−21​θ2T).

It is positive, and its P\mathbb{P}P-mean is 1, because E[e−θWT]=e12θ2T\mathbb{E}[e^{-\theta W_T}] = e^{\frac12\theta^2 T}E[e−θWT​]=e21​θ2T for a normal variable with variance TTT. Multiply the normal density of WTW_TWT​ by ZZZ and complete the square in the exponent:

12πT e−w22T⋅e−θw−12θ2T=12πT e−(w+θT)22T.\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi T}}\,e^{-\frac{w^2}{2T}} \cdot e^{-\theta w - \frac12\theta^2 T} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi T}}\,e^{-\frac{(w + \theta T)^2}{2T}}.2πT​1​e−2Tw2​⋅e−θw−21​θ2T=2πT​1​e−2T(w+θT)2​.

The result is again a normal density with the same variance TTT, but with mean −θT-\theta T−θT. Reweighting by ZZZ has moved the distribution sideways without changing its shape. Outcomes on one side get weights above 1, outcomes on the other side weights below 1.

Girsanov's theorem

The same reweighting works for the whole path, not just the endpoint. Girsanov's theorem states: if WWW is a Brownian motion under P\mathbb{P}P and Q\mathbb{Q}Q is defined by the density ZZZ above, then

W~t=Wt+θt\tilde W_t = W_t + \theta tW~t​=Wt​+θt

is a Brownian motion under Q\mathbb{Q}Q. The paths themselves are unchanged. Under the new weights, WWW acquires a drift of −θ-\theta−θ per unit time, and adding θt\theta tθt back removes it. In increments, dWt=dW~t−θ dtdW_t = d\tilde W_t - \theta\,dtdWt​=dW~t​−θdt: a change of measure changes the drift of a process and leaves its volatility alone.

Geometric Brownian motion under the risk-neutral measure

Take the stock model from "Geometric Brownian motion", dSt=μSt dt+σSt dWtdS_t = \mu S_t\,dt + \sigma S_t\,dW_tdSt​=μSt​dt+σSt​dWt​, and substitute dWt=dW~t−θ dtdW_t = d\tilde W_t - \theta\,dtdWt​=dW~t​−θdt:

dSt=(μ−σθ) St dt+σSt dW~t.dS_t = (\mu - \sigma\theta)\,S_t\,dt + \sigma S_t\,d\tilde W_t.dSt​=(μ−σθ)St​dt+σSt​dW~t​.

Choose θ\thetaθ so that the drift becomes the risk-free rate rrr:

θ=μ−rσ,dSt=rSt dt+σSt dW~t.\theta = \frac{\mu - r}{\sigma}, \qquad dS_t = r S_t\,dt + \sigma S_t\,d\tilde W_t.θ=σμ−r​,dSt​=rSt​dt+σSt​dW~t​.

The number θ\thetaθ is the market price of risk: the excess return of the stock per unit of volatility. Under Q\mathbb{Q}Q the stock earns rrr on average, the continuous-time version of EQ[S1]=(1+r)S0\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[S_1] = (1+r)S_0EQ[S1​]=(1+r)S0​ on the tree. By Itô's lemma, as in "Itô's lemma",

ln⁡STS0∼N ⁣((μ−12σ2)T, σ2T) under P,ln⁡STS0∼N ⁣((r−12σ2)T, σ2T) under Q.\ln\frac{S_T}{S_0} \sim N\!\left(\left(\mu - \tfrac12\sigma^2\right)T,\ \sigma^2 T\right) \text{ under } \mathbb{P}, \qquad \ln\frac{S_T}{S_0} \sim N\!\left(\left(r - \tfrac12\sigma^2\right)T,\ \sigma^2 T\right) \text{ under } \mathbb{Q}.lnS0​ST​​∼N((μ−21​σ2)T, σ2T) under P,lnS0​ST​​∼N((r−21​σ2)T, σ2T) under Q.

This explains why option prices depend on σ\sigmaσ but not on μ\muμ. A price is a discounted Q\mathbb{Q}Q-expectation, and under Q\mathbb{Q}Q the drift is rrr whatever μ\muμ was. The volatility survives the change of measure, because Girsanov only shifts the mean.

Summary

  • Two measures on the same outcomes differ only in their weights. The density Z=dQ/dPZ = d\mathbb{Q}/d\mathbb{P}Z=dQ/dP converts one into the other: EQ[X]=EP[ZX]\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[X] = \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[ZX]EQ[X]=EP[ZX].
  • Equivalent measures share their impossible events, so they agree on what counts as an arbitrage.
  • Reweighting a normal variable by e−θWT−12θ2Te^{-\theta W_T - \frac12\theta^2 T}e−θWT​−21​θ2T shifts its mean by −θT-\theta T−θT and keeps its variance.
  • Girsanov: W~t=Wt+θt\tilde W_t = W_t + \theta tW~t​=Wt​+θt is a Q\mathbb{Q}Q-Brownian motion. With θ=(μ−r)/σ\theta = (\mu - r)/\sigmaθ=(μ−r)/σ the stock drifts at rrr under Q\mathbb{Q}Q.
  • Prices are Q\mathbb{Q}Q-expectations, so they depend on σ\sigmaσ but not on μ\muμ.
