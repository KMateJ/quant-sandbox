Itô's lemma

Lesson 10 of 17 · 1 min read

The lesson "Random walks and Brownian motion" showed that Brownian paths have no slope anywhere, so ordinary calculus does not apply to them directly. This lesson develops the replacement, Itô's lemma, the chain rule for functions of a random process. Its key ingredient is a surprising fact about squared increments of Brownian motion.

Increments in continuous time

Write dWtdW_tdWt​ for the increment of a Brownian motion over a short interval dtdtdt. From the definition of Brownian motion it is normal with mean zero and variance dtdtdt:

dWt∼N(0, dt),E[dWt]=0,E[(dWt)2]=dt.dW_t \sim N(0,\ dt), \qquad \mathbb{E}[dW_t] = 0, \qquad \mathbb{E}\big[(dW_t)^2\big] = dt.dWt​∼N(0, dt),E[dWt​]=0,E[(dWt​)2]=dt.

A process driven by Brownian motion is described by its increment too. The general Itô process has a drift aaa and a volatility bbb, which may depend on time and on the current value:

dXt=a(t,Xt) dt+b(t,Xt) dWt.dX_t = a(t, X_t)\,dt + b(t, X_t)\,dW_t.dXt​=a(t,Xt​)dt+b(t,Xt​)dWt​.

Read it as a recipe for one small step: a deterministic move a dta\,dtadt plus a random move b dWtb\,dW_tbdWt​ whose typical size is bdtb\sqrt{dt}bdt​. For small dtdtdt the random part is much larger than the drift part, since dt\sqrt{dt}dt​ is much larger than dtdtdt.

Quadratic variation

In ordinary calculus, squares of small increments are negligible: if dxdxdx is small, (dx)2(dx)^2(dx)2 is much smaller and disappears in the limit. For Brownian motion this fails. Split [0,T][0, T][0,T] into nnn steps of length Δt=T/n\Delta t = T/nΔt=T/n and add up the squared increments:

Qn=∑i=1n(Wti−Wti−1)2.Q_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \big(W_{t_i} - W_{t_{i-1}}\big)^2.Qn​=i=1∑n​(Wti​​−Wti−1​​)2.

Each squared increment has mean Δt\Delta tΔt, so E[Qn]=n Δt=T\mathbb{E}[Q_n] = n\,\Delta t = TE[Qn​]=nΔt=T for every nnn. Each one also has variance 2 Δt22\,\Delta t^22Δt2 (a property of the normal distribution), and the increments are independent, so

Var⁡(Qn)=n⋅2 Δt2=2T2n⟶0as n→∞.\operatorname{Var}(Q_n) = n \cdot 2\,\Delta t^2 = \frac{2T^2}{n} \longrightarrow 0 \quad \text{as } n \to \infty.Var(Qn​)=n⋅2Δt2=n2T2​⟶0as n→∞.

The sum keeps its mean TTT while its randomness disappears: in the limit it equals TTT exactly. This limit is the quadratic variation of Brownian motion. Example: on [0,1][0,1][0,1] the standard deviation of QnQ_nQn​ is 2/n\sqrt{2/n}2/n​, which is 0.4470.4470.447 for n=10n = 10n=10, 0.1410.1410.141 for n=100n = 100n=100 and 0.04470.04470.0447 for n=1000n = 1000n=1000.

For a smooth path the same sum goes to zero, because each squared increment is of order Δt2\Delta t^2Δt2. Brownian motion is rough enough that its squared increments add up to something finite.

The sum of the absolute increments behaves very differently. Each ∣ΔW∣|\Delta W|∣ΔW∣ has mean 2Δt/π\sqrt{2\Delta t/\pi}2Δt/π​, so on [0,1][0,1][0,1] the total variation ∑∣ΔW∣\sum |\Delta W|∑∣ΔW∣ has mean n2/(πn)=2n/πn\sqrt{2/(\pi n)} = \sqrt{2n/\pi}n2/(πn)​=2n/π​: about 2.522.522.52 for n=10n = 10n=10, 7.987.987.98 for n=100n = 100n=100 and 25.2325.2325.23 for n=1000n = 1000n=1000. It grows without bound. The length travelled by a Brownian path is infinite, yet the sum of its squared steps settles down to elapsed time.

The result is written as a set of multiplication rules for small increments:

(dWt)2=dt,dt dWt=0,(dt)2=0.(dW_t)^2 = dt, \qquad dt\,dW_t = 0, \qquad (dt)^2 = 0.(dWt​)2=dt,dtdWt​=0,(dt)2=0.

Quadratic variation: the squared increments of Brownian motion over [0,T][0,T][0,T] add up to TTT. In shorthand, (dWt)2=dt(dW_t)^2 = dt(dWt​)2=dt.

Itô's lemma

Let f(t,x)f(t, x)f(t,x) be a smooth function and XtX_tXt​ an Itô process as above. We want the increment of Yt=f(t,Xt)Y_t = f(t, X_t)Yt​=f(t,Xt​). Expand fff to second order in a Taylor series:

df=∂f∂t dt+∂f∂x dXt+12∂2f∂x2 (dXt)2+⋯df = \frac{\partial f}{\partial t}\,dt + \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\,dX_t + \frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2}\,(dX_t)^2 + \cdotsdf=∂t∂f​dt+∂x∂f​dXt​+21​∂x2∂2f​(dXt​)2+⋯

In ordinary calculus the third term would be dropped. Here it must be kept. Square dXt=a dt+b dWtdX_t = a\,dt + b\,dW_tdXt​=adt+bdWt​ using the multiplication rules: the (dt)2(dt)^2(dt)2 and dt dWtdt\,dW_tdtdWt​ terms vanish and only b2(dWt)2=b2 dtb^2 (dW_t)^2 = b^2\,dtb2(dWt​)2=b2dt survives:

(dXt)2=b2 dt.(dX_t)^2 = b^2\,dt.(dXt​)2=b2dt.

Higher-order terms are of order dt3/2dt^{3/2}dt3/2 or smaller and vanish. Substituting gives Itô's lemma:

df(t,Xt)=(∂f∂t+a ∂f∂x+12 b2 ∂2f∂x2)dt+b ∂f∂x dWt.df(t, X_t) = \left( \frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + a\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} + \frac{1}{2}\,b^2\,\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2} \right) dt + b\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\,dW_t.df(t,Xt​)=(∂t∂f​+a∂x∂f​+21​b2∂x2∂2f​)dt+b∂x∂f​dWt​.

Compared with the ordinary chain rule there is one extra drift term, 12b2f′′\tfrac12 b^2 f''21​b2f′′. It is the price of the roughness of Brownian paths: the curvature of fff turns the random fluctuations of XXX into a systematic drift of f(X)f(X)f(X).

The square of Brownian motion

Take Xt=WtX_t = W_tXt​=Wt​ (so a=0a = 0a=0, b=1b = 1b=1) and f(x)=x2f(x) = x^2f(x)=x2, with f′=2xf' = 2xf′=2x and f′′=2f'' = 2f′′=2. Itô's lemma gives

d(Wt2)=dt+2Wt dWt.d\big(W_t^2\big) = dt + 2W_t\,dW_t.d(Wt2​)=dt+2Wt​dWt​.

Ordinary calculus would give only 2Wt dWt2W_t\,dW_t2Wt​dWt​. The random term has mean zero, so taking expectations of the accumulated increments leaves E[Wt2]=t\mathbb{E}[W_t^2] = tE[Wt2​]=t, which agrees with Var⁡(Wt)=t\operatorname{Var}(W_t) = tVar(Wt​)=t. Without the extra dtdtdt the expectation would wrongly stay at zero.

The logarithm of a stock price

The model of "Geometric Brownian motion" is dSt=μSt dt+σSt dWtdS_t = \mu S_t\,dt + \sigma S_t\,dW_tdSt​=μSt​dt+σSt​dWt​, an Itô process with a=μSa = \mu Sa=μS and b=σSb = \sigma Sb=σS. Apply Itô's lemma to f(S)=ln⁡Sf(S) = \ln Sf(S)=lnS, with f′=1/Sf' = 1/Sf′=1/S and f′′=−1/S2f'' = -1/S^2f′′=−1/S2:

dln⁡St=(μS⋅1S+12 σ2S2⋅(−1S2))dt+σS⋅1S dWt=(μ−12σ2)dt+σ dWt.d\ln S_t = \left( \mu S \cdot \frac{1}{S} + \frac{1}{2}\,\sigma^2 S^2 \cdot \left(-\frac{1}{S^2}\right) \right) dt + \sigma S \cdot \frac{1}{S}\,dW_t = \left(\mu - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right) dt + \sigma\,dW_t.dlnSt​=(μS⋅S1​+21​σ2S2⋅(−S21​))dt+σS⋅S1​dWt​=(μ−21​σ2)dt+σdWt​.

The right-hand side has constant coefficients, so it can be added up over [0,T][0, T][0,T] directly:

ln⁡ST=ln⁡S0+(μ−12σ2)T+σWT.\ln S_T = \ln S_0 + \left(\mu - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right) T + \sigma W_T.lnST​=lnS0​+(μ−21​σ2)T+σWT​.

This is the solution quoted in "Geometric Brownian motion", and the −12σ2-\tfrac12\sigma^2−21​σ2 correction is exactly Itô's extra term. Naive calculus would have predicted a log drift of μ\muμ.

The naive line ln⁡100+0.8\ln 100 + 0.8ln100+0.8 would point to 100 e0.8≈222.55100\,e^{0.8} \approx 222.55100e0.8≈222.55, which is the mean price, not where a typical path ends.

Size of the correction

Take one day, Δt=1/252\Delta t = 1/252Δt=1/252, with σ=20%\sigma = 20\%σ=20%. The random part of the return, σ ΔW\sigma\,\Delta WσΔW, is typically about 0.2×1/252≈1.26%0.2 \times \sqrt{1/252} \approx 1.26\%0.2×1/252​≈1.26%. Its square is about σ2Δt≈0.016%\sigma^2 \Delta t \approx 0.016\%σ2Δt≈0.016%, the same order of magnitude as the daily drift μ Δt≈0.032%\mu\,\Delta t \approx 0.032\%μΔt≈0.032%. Adding up such squares day after day does not average out, because they are never negative. That is why the second-order term changes the drift and cannot be ignored.

Summary

  • An Itô process moves by dX=a dt+b dWdX = a\,dt + b\,dWdX=adt+bdW: a drift step plus a random step of size about bdtb\sqrt{dt}bdt​.
  • Squared Brownian increments add up to elapsed time: (dW)2=dt(dW)^2 = dt(dW)2=dt, while dt dWdt\,dWdtdW and (dt)2(dt)^2(dt)2 vanish.
  • Itô's lemma: df=(ft+afx+12b2fxx) dt+bfx dWdf = (f_t + a f_x + \tfrac12 b^2 f_{xx})\,dt + b f_x\,dWdf=(ft​+afx​+21​b2fxx​)dt+bfx​dW. The term 12b2fxx\tfrac12 b^2 f_{xx}21​b2fxx​ is new compared with ordinary calculus.
  • Applied to ln⁡S\ln SlnS under GBM it gives dln⁡S=(μ−12σ2) dt+σ dWd\ln S = (\mu - \tfrac12\sigma^2)\,dt + \sigma\,dWdlnS=(μ−21​σ2)dt+σdW.
