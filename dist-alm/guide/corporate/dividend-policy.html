Dividends, payout policy and the dividend discount model

Lesson 7 of 10 · 1 min read

A share entitles its owner to the dividends the firm will pay. Valuing it is therefore a discounting problem of the kind solved in "Interest, compounding and discounting", and with steady growth the sum collapses to the growing perpetuity of "Annuities, perpetuities and loan amortization". This lesson derives that formula, shows where the growth comes from, and asks the question that "Capital structure and the Modigliani–Miller theorems" asked about debt, now about payout: does it matter how much of its earnings a firm pays out?

Dividends and the dividend discount model

Let DtD_tDt​ be the dividend per share expected at the end of year ttt, and kkk the return shareholders require on a share of this risk, the cost of equity. The price today is the present value of all future dividends, the dividend discount model:

P0=∑t=1∞Dt(1+k)t.P_0 = \sum_{t=1}^{\infty} \frac{D_t}{(1+k)^t}.P0​=t=1∑∞​(1+k)tDt​​.

A share has no maturity, so the sum is infinite. It may seem that an investor who plans to sell after a year is buying the dividend and the sale price, not an infinite stream; but the sale price is itself the present value of the dividends from year 2 on, so the two views agree.

The Gordon growth formula

If dividends grow at a constant rate ggg, so that Dt=D1(1+g)t−1D_t = D_1 (1+g)^{t-1}Dt​=D1​(1+g)t−1, the sum is a growing perpetuity. Provided g<kg < kg<k it converges to the Gordon growth formula:

P0=D1k−g.P_0 = \frac{D_1}{k - g}.P0​=k−gD1​​.

The formula is simple, and it is very sensitive near g=kg = kg=k: as the growth rate approaches the discount rate the denominator goes to zero and the price explodes. For g≥kg \ge kg≥k the sum diverges and the formula has no meaning; no firm can grow faster than the discount rate for ever. Rearranged, k=D1/P0+gk = D_1/P_0 + gk=D1​/P0​+g: the required return is the dividend yield plus the growth rate.

Growth from retained earnings

Growth is not free. A firm grows its earnings by reinvesting part of them. Let E1E_1E1​ be next year's earnings per share (EPS), ddd the payout ratio (the share of earnings paid as dividends) and b=1−db = 1 - db=1−d the retention ratio. If retained earnings are invested at a return on equity ROE, earnings and dividends grow at

g=b⋅ROE,D1=d⋅E1,P0=d E1k−b ROE.g = b \cdot \text{ROE}, \qquad D_1 = d \cdot E_1, \qquad P_0 = \frac{d\,E_1}{k - b\,\text{ROE}}.g=b⋅ROE,D1​=d⋅E1​,P0​=k−bROEdE1​​.

Here is the trade-off. Retaining more lowers today's dividend but raises the growth rate. Which effect wins depends only on how ROE compares with kkk. If the firm earns more on reinvested money than shareholders require, retention creates value and the price rises as the payout falls. If it earns less, retention destroys value, and shareholders would be better off receiving the cash.

Payout irrelevance

Now set ROE equal to kkk. The price becomes dE1/(k−(1−d)k)=E1/kd E_1 / (k - (1-d)k) = E_1/kdE1​/(k−(1−d)k)=E1​/k for every payout ratio: in the figure the curve sits on the flat line. Reinvesting at exactly the required return neither creates nor destroys value, so how earnings are split between dividends and reinvestment does not matter.

Miller and Modigliani showed that this holds far more generally. In perfect capital markets (no taxes, no transaction costs, no information asymmetry) and with the investment policy fixed, the payout policy does not affect the value of the firm. The argument is the same as for capital structure: investors can undo any policy themselves. A shareholder who wants more cash than the firm pays can sell a few shares, a homemade dividend; one who wants less can reinvest the dividend in more shares. Since anyone can build the payout they want at no cost, no one pays extra for a particular policy.

The same reasoning covers share buybacks. Paying 100 as a dividend or using it to repurchase shares hands shareholders the same cash; the dividend leaves every holder with the same number of shares at a lower price, the buyback leaves fewer shares at an unchanged price. In perfect markets the two are equivalent.

When payout matters

Taxes. Where dividends are taxed more heavily than capital gains, or the gain can be deferred until a sale, cash paid as a dividend is worth less to the shareholder than the same cash returned through a buyback. Different tax positions also attract different investors to different policies, the so-called clientele effect.

Signalling. Managers know more about future earnings than outside investors. Because firms are reluctant to cut dividends, raising one is a costly and therefore credible signal that management expects earnings to hold up, and prices often react to dividend announcements accordingly.

Agency. Cash left inside the firm can be spent on projects that serve managers rather than shareholders. Committing to pay it out reduces that risk, which is one reason mature firms with few good projects pay high dividends.

The present value of growth opportunities

The Gordon price can be split into the value of the current earnings, paid out for ever, and the rest:

P0=E1k+PVGO.P_0 = \frac{E_1}{k} + \text{PVGO}.P0​=kE1​​+PVGO.

The present value of growth opportunities (PVGO) is the part of the price that comes from future investments. It is positive only if those investments earn more than kkk; it is zero when ROE equals kkk, however much the firm reinvests; and it is negative when ROE is below kkk and the firm keeps reinvesting. A high PVGO share is a growth stock whose price depends on projects it has not yet made.

The dividend discount model values equity directly. The discounted cash flow valuation later in this track values the whole firm from its free cash flows and uses the same Gordon formula for the terminal value.

Summary

  • The dividend discount model prices a share as the present value of all future dividends at the cost of equity kkk.
  • With constant growth g<kg < kg<k it reduces to the Gordon formula P0=D1/(k−g)P_0 = D_1/(k - g)P0​=D1​/(k−g).
  • Growth comes from retained earnings, g=b⋅ROEg = b \cdot \text{ROE}g=b⋅ROE; retention raises the price if and only if ROE exceeds kkk.
  • With ROE = kkk and perfect markets, payout is irrelevant: homemade dividends and buybacks undo any policy.
  • Taxes, signalling and agency costs are the reasons payout matters in practice.
  • PVGO =P0−E1/k= P_0 - E_1/k=P0​−E1​/k is the value of future growth; at the defaults it is 21.43 of a 71.43 price.
