Discounted cash flow valuation

Lesson 8 of 10 · 1 min read

The lesson "Net present value and internal rate of return" valued a single project by discounting its cash flows. A whole firm can be valued the same way: it is a collection of projects that keeps producing cash. This lesson builds a discounted cash flow (DCF) valuation from the firm's free cash flows, the WACC from "The weighted average cost of capital", and a formula for cash flows that continue forever.

Free cash flow

The cash flow to discount is the free cash flow (FCF): the cash the firm's operations generate in a year after paying taxes and making the investments needed to keep growing, but before any payments to lenders or shareholders. With ttt the corporate tax rate,

FCF=EBIT (1−t)+depreciation−capital expenditure−ΔNWC.\text{FCF} = \text{EBIT}\,(1 - t) + \text{depreciation} - \text{capital expenditure} - \Delta\text{NWC}.FCF=EBIT(1−t)+depreciation−capital expenditure−ΔNWC.

Each term has a reason. EBIT (earnings before interest and taxes) is operating profit; EBIT(1−t)\text{EBIT}(1-t)EBIT(1−t) is that profit after the tax the operations would pay with no debt, so financing is kept out of the cash flow, as "The weighted average cost of capital" required. Depreciation is an accounting charge, not a cash payment, so it is added back. Capital expenditure is cash spent on new equipment and buildings. ΔNWC\Delta\text{NWC}ΔNWC is the increase in net working capital (inventories plus receivables minus payables), cash tied up in day-to-day operations.

Example: EBIT of 200, t=25%t = 25\%t=25%, depreciation of 40, capital expenditure of 60 and an increase in working capital of 10 give FCF=150+40−60−10=120\text{FCF} = 150 + 40 - 60 - 10 = 120FCF=150+40−60−10=120.

Enterprise value

Free cash flow belongs to all investors together, lenders and shareholders. Discounting it at the WACC therefore gives the value of the whole firm, its enterprise value:

EV=∑k=1∞FCFk(1+WACC)k.\text{EV} = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{\text{FCF}_k}{(1 + \text{WACC})^k}.EV=k=1∑∞​(1+WACC)kFCFk​​.

The sum has no end, because a firm has no fixed maturity. In practice it is split in two: an explicit forecast of the next few years, usually five, where the analyst projects each cash flow, and a terminal value that summarises everything after.

Growing perpetuity

To value the cash flows beyond the forecast, assume that from some point they grow at a constant rate ggg forever: FCFk+1=FCFk (1+g)\text{FCF}_{k+1} = \text{FCF}_k\,(1+g)FCFk+1​=FCFk​(1+g). Write WWW for the WACC. The present value of such a stream, starting with FCF1\text{FCF}_1FCF1​ in one year, is a geometric series with ratio (1+g)/(1+W)(1+g)/(1+W)(1+g)/(1+W):

∑k=1∞FCF1(1+g)k−1(1+W)k=FCF11+W∑j=0∞(1+g1+W)j=FCF1W−g.\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{\text{FCF}_1 (1+g)^{k-1}}{(1+W)^k} = \frac{\text{FCF}_1}{1+W} \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \Big(\frac{1+g}{1+W}\Big)^j = \frac{\text{FCF}_1}{W - g}.k=1∑∞​(1+W)kFCF1​(1+g)k−1​=1+WFCF1​​j=0∑∞​(1+W1+g​)j=W−gFCF1​​.

The series converges only when g<Wg < Wg<W. This is the Gordon growth formula. A growth rate at or above the discount rate would give an infinite value, which signals an impossible assumption: no firm can outgrow the economy forever.

Terminal value

Apply the formula at the end of the forecast. If the forecast covers years 1 to 5, the terminal value at year 5 is the value then of all cash flows from year 6 on:

TV5=FCF6W−g,EV=∑k=15FCFk(1+W)k+TV5(1+W)5.\text{TV}_5 = \frac{\text{FCF}_6}{W - g}, \qquad \text{EV} = \sum_{k=1}^{5} \frac{\text{FCF}_k}{(1+W)^k} + \frac{\text{TV}_5}{(1+W)^5}.TV5​=W−gFCF6​​,EV=k=1∑5​(1+W)kFCFk​​+(1+W)5TV5​​.

The terminal value is itself a future amount, so it is discounted back five years like any other cash flow.

A worked example

Take FCF1=10\text{FCF}_1 = 10FCF1​=10, growing at g=2%g = 2\%g=2% per year forever, and W=9%W = 9\%W=9%. The forecast cash flows are 10, 10.20, 10.40, 10.61 and 10.82; discounted at 9% they are worth 9.17, 8.59, 8.03, 7.52 and 7.04.

Most of a DCF value usually sits in the terminal value. In the example it is 102.51/142.86≈71.8%102.51/142.86 \approx 71.8\%102.51/142.86≈71.8% of the total, so the long-run assumptions on ggg and the WACC matter more than the detailed forecast.

Sensitivity to the assumptions

Because W−gW - gW−g sits in a denominator, small changes in either have large effects. Raising ggg from 2% to 4% lifts the value from 142.86 to 10/0.05=20010/0.05 = 20010/0.05=200; lowering the WACC from 9% to 8% lifts it to 10/0.06≈166.6710/0.06 \approx 166.6710/0.06≈166.67. The present value of the five forecast years barely moves in either case. A DCF valuation should therefore always be reported with a range of ggg and WACC values, not as a single number.

From enterprise value to equity value

Enterprise value belongs to lenders and shareholders together. The shareholders' part, the equity value, is what remains after subtracting net debt (debt minus cash):

equity value=EV−(debt−cash),value per share=equity valuenumber of shares.\text{equity value} = \text{EV} - (\text{debt} - \text{cash}), \qquad \text{value per share} = \frac{\text{equity value}}{\text{number of shares}}.equity value=EV−(debt−cash),value per share=number of sharesequity value​.

Example: with EV=142.86\text{EV} = 142.86EV=142.86, debt of 45, cash of 5 and 10 shares, the equity value is 142.86−40=102.86142.86 - 40 = 102.86142.86−40=102.86, or about 10.29 per share. Comparing this with the market price is how an analyst decides whether a share looks cheap or expensive.

Summary

  • Free cash flow is the cash operations produce after tax and investment, before any financing payments.
  • Discounting free cash flow at the WACC gives the enterprise value of the firm.
  • A stream growing at g<Wg < Wg<W forever is worth FCF1/(W−g)\text{FCF}_1/(W - g)FCF1​/(W−g); applied at the end of the forecast, this is the terminal value.
  • The terminal value usually dominates, so results are very sensitive to ggg and the WACC.
  • Equity value is enterprise value minus net debt.
