Capital structure and the Modigliani–Miller theorems

Lesson 6 of 10 · 1 min read

The lesson "The weighted average cost of capital" took the mix of debt and equity as given. A firm can change that mix: borrow and buy back shares, or issue shares and repay debt. This lesson asks whether doing so changes the value of the firm and its cost of capital. The answer, due to Modigliani and Miller, is no in a world without taxes, and yes, through the tax shield, once corporate taxes are included.

Capital structure and leverage

A firm's capital structure is its mix of debt DDD and equity EEE. It is described by the debt ratio D/VD/VD/V, with V=D+EV = D + EV=D+E, or equivalently by the debt-to-equity ratio D/ED/ED/E. The two are linked by

DE=D/V1−D/V.\frac{D}{E} = \frac{D/V}{1 - D/V}.ED​=1−D/VD/V​.

A firm that uses debt is called levered; the same firm with no debt is unlevered. Using more debt is called increasing leverage. Throughout the lesson the firm's operations, and therefore its operating cash flows, stay fixed. Only the way those cash flows are split between lenders and shareholders changes.

Assumptions

Modigliani and Miller work in an idealised market. State the assumptions explicitly, because each conclusion below depends on them:

  • No taxes (relaxed later in the lesson).
  • No costs of bankruptcy or financial distress.
  • Investors and firms can borrow and lend at the same rate.
  • Everyone has the same information, and there are no transaction costs.

Firm value without taxes

Let VUV_UVU​ be the value of the unlevered firm and VLV_LVL​ the value of the same firm with debt. Modigliani–Miller Proposition I states that without taxes

VL=VU.V_L = V_U.VL​=VU​.

The argument is a no-arbitrage argument. Owning all the debt and all the equity of the levered firm gives exactly the operating cash flows of the firm, which are the same with or without debt. Two claims to the same cash flows must have the same price. A shareholder who wants more leverage can also borrow personally and buy more shares ("homemade leverage"), so the firm cannot create value by doing the borrowing for them.

A fair picture is a pizza: cutting it into more or fewer slices does not change how much pizza there is.

The cost of equity rises with leverage

Write rUr_UrU​ for the return investors require on the unlevered firm, the unlevered cost of capital. It reflects only the business risk of the operations. Since VL=VUV_L = V_UVL​=VU​ and the cash flows are unchanged, the return on the whole levered firm is still rUr_UrU​, so its WACC (without taxes) equals rUr_UrU​:

rU=EV rE+DV rD.r_U = \frac{E}{V}\,r_E + \frac{D}{V}\,r_D.rU​=VE​rE​+VD​rD​.

Multiply by V/EV/EV/E and solve for the cost of equity rEr_ErE​:

rE=rU VE−rD DE=rU(1+DE)−rD DE=rU+(rU−rD) DE.r_E = r_U\,\frac{V}{E} - r_D\,\frac{D}{E} = r_U\Big(1 + \frac{D}{E}\Big) - r_D\,\frac{D}{E} = r_U + (r_U - r_D)\,\frac{D}{E}.rE​=rU​EV​−rD​ED​=rU​(1+ED​)−rD​ED​=rU​+(rU​−rD​)ED​.

This is Modigliani–Miller Proposition II. Debt is cheaper than equity, rD<rUr_D < r_UrD​<rU​, but borrowing more does not lower the WACC: the shareholders bear the same business risk on a smaller stake, so their required return rEr_ErE​ rises exactly enough to offset the cheaper debt.

Example: rU=10%r_U = 10\%rU​=10%, rD=5%r_D = 5\%rD​=5% and D/V=40%D/V = 40\%D/V=40%, so D/E=0.4/0.6≈0.667D/E = 0.4/0.6 \approx 0.667D/E=0.4/0.6≈0.667. Then rE=10%+5%×0.667≈13.33%r_E = 10\% + 5\% \times 0.667 \approx 13.33\%rE​=10%+5%×0.667≈13.33%, and the WACC is 0.6×13.33%+0.4×5%=10%=rU0.6 \times 13.33\% + 0.4 \times 5\% = 10\% = r_U0.6×13.33%+0.4×5%=10%=rU​.

Without taxes, capital structure is irrelevant: VL=VUV_L = V_UVL​=VU​, the WACC equals rUr_UrU​ at every debt ratio, and rE=rU+(rU−rD) D/Er_E = r_U + (r_U - r_D)\,D/ErE​=rU​+(rU​−rD​)D/E.

Corporate taxes and the tax shield

Now add a corporate tax rate ttt. As in "The weighted average cost of capital", interest is deductible, so a firm with debt DDD at rate rDr_DrD​ saves t rDDt\,r_D DtrD​D in tax every year. Assume the debt is perpetual: the firm keeps the amount DDD outstanding forever. The tax savings are then a perpetuity, and discounting it at rDr_DrD​ (it is as safe as the interest payments) gives the value of the tax shield:

PV(tax shield)=t rDDrD=t D,VL=VU+t D.\text{PV(tax shield)} = \frac{t\,r_D D}{r_D} = t\,D, \qquad V_L = V_U + t\,D.PV(tax shield)=rD​trD​D​=tD,VL​=VU​+tD.

Example: an unlevered firm worth VU=1000V_U = 1000VU​=1000 that borrows D=400D = 400D=400 at t=25%t = 25\%t=25% is worth VL=1000+0.25×400=1100V_L = 1000 + 0.25 \times 400 = 1100VL​=1000+0.25×400=1100. The extra 100 is money that no longer goes to the tax authority.

The formulas for the costs of capital change accordingly. With perpetual debt they become

rE=rU+(rU−rD)(1−t) DE,WACC=EV rE+DV rD(1−t)=rU(1−t DV).r_E = r_U + (r_U - r_D)(1 - t)\,\frac{D}{E}, \qquad \text{WACC} = \frac{E}{V}\,r_E + \frac{D}{V}\,r_D(1 - t) = r_U\Big(1 - t\,\frac{D}{V}\Big).rE​=rU​+(rU​−rD​)(1−t)ED​,WACC=VE​rE​+VD​rD​(1−t)=rU​(1−tVD​).

Limits of the result

Taken literally, the version with taxes says a firm should be financed almost entirely by debt. Real firms are not, because the assumptions fail at high leverage. Heavily indebted firms face costs of financial distress: higher borrowing rates, lost customers and suppliers, legal costs in bankruptcy. The trade-off view of capital structure balances the tax shield against these costs, which suggests a moderate debt ratio at which firm value is highest. The figure and the lab show only the tax side of this balance.

Summary

  • Capital structure is the mix of debt and equity, measured by D/VD/VD/V or D/ED/ED/E; operating cash flows do not depend on it.
  • Without taxes, VL=VUV_L = V_UVL​=VU​ and the WACC equals the unlevered cost of capital rUr_UrU​ at any leverage (Proposition I).
  • The cost of equity rises with leverage: rE=rU+(rU−rD) D/Er_E = r_U + (r_U - r_D)\,D/ErE​=rU​+(rU​−rD​)D/E (Proposition II).
  • With corporate taxes and perpetual debt, VL=VU+tDV_L = V_U + tDVL​=VU​+tD, rE=rU+(rU−rD)(1−t) D/Er_E = r_U + (r_U - r_D)(1 - t)\,D/ErE​=rU​+(rU​−rD​)(1−t)D/E and WACC=rU(1−t D/V)\text{WACC} = r_U(1 - t\,D/V)WACC=rU​(1−tD/V).
  • Costs of financial distress, which the model leaves out, limit how much debt is worth taking on.
