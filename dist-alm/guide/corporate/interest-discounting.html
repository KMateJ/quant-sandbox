Interest, compounding and discounting

Lesson 1 of 10 · 1 min read

Almost every pricing formula in finance compares money at different dates. Before options, portfolios or bonds, we need one tool: a way to move an amount of money forward or backward in time. This lesson builds that tool.

The time value of money

If you have 100 today, you can put it in a bank account (or buy a government bill) and have more than 100 in a year, with essentially no risk. So 100 today is worth more than 100 promised in a year. The extra amount you are paid for waiting is interest, and the rate at which it accrues is the interest rate rrr, quoted per year.

Throughout these lessons rrr means the risk-free rate: the rate on a loan that is certain to be repaid. Risky lending earns more, but that extra is compensation for risk, not for time.

Simple interest

With simple interest you earn rrr on the original amount only. After TTT years an initial amount B0B_0B0​ has grown to

BT=B0 (1+rT).B_T = B_0\,(1 + rT).BT​=B0​(1+rT).

Growth is linear in time. This is how some short-term money market instruments are quoted, but it ignores the fact that interest already earned could itself earn interest.

Compound interest

With annual compounding, the interest earned in year one is added to the balance, and in year two you earn interest on the larger amount. After TTT whole years:

BT=B0 (1+r)T.B_T = B_0\,(1 + r)^T.BT​=B0​(1+r)T.

If interest is credited mmm times per year, each period pays r/mr/mr/m and there are mTmTmT periods:

BT=B0(1+rm)mT.B_T = B_0\left(1 + \frac{r}{m}\right)^{mT}.BT​=B0​(1+mr​)mT.

A snowball is a fair picture: the bigger it gets, the more snow it picks up per turn. More frequent compounding gives slightly more growth, because interest starts earning interest sooner.

Continuous compounding

Let the number of compounding periods mmm grow without limit. The expression above converges to an exponential:

lim⁡m→∞(1+rm)mT=erT,BT=B0 erT.\lim_{m\to\infty}\left(1 + \frac{r}{m}\right)^{mT} = e^{rT}, \qquad B_T = B_0\,e^{rT}.m→∞lim​(1+mr​)mT=erT,BT​=B0​erT.

Continuous compounding is the convention used in option pricing (Black–Scholes, binomial trees with small steps) because exponentials are easy to multiply and differentiate: growing for T1T_1T1​ and then for T2T_2T2​ is the same as growing for T1+T2T_1 + T_2T1​+T2​, since erT1erT2=er(T1+T2)e^{rT_1}e^{rT_2} = e^{r(T_1+T_2)}erT1​erT2​=er(T1​+T2​).

The figure compares the three conventions for the same quoted rate. At low rates and short horizons they are almost identical; the gap opens up as rrr and TTT grow.

Discounting

Discounting is compounding run backwards. If a payment CCC arrives at time TTT, its present value is the amount you would need to invest today to have exactly CCC at TTT:

PV=C e−rT(continuous),PV=C(1+r)T(annual).\text{PV} = C\,e^{-rT} \quad\text{(continuous)}, \qquad \text{PV} = \frac{C}{(1+r)^T} \quad\text{(annual)}.PV=Ce−rT(continuous),PV=(1+r)TC​(annual).

The multiplier e−rTe^{-rT}e−rT is called the discount factor. It is always between 0 and 1 for a positive rate, and it shrinks the further away the payment is.

Example: with r=5%r = 5\%r=5% and continuous compounding, 100 paid in two years is worth 100 e−0.1≈90.48100\,e^{-0.1} \approx 90.48100e−0.1≈90.48 today. Investing 90.48 at 5% for two years gives back exactly 100.

Converting between conventions

The same growth can be quoted in different conventions. A continuously compounded rate rcr_crc​ and an annually compounded rate rar_ara​ describe the same growth when

erc=1+ra⟺rc=ln⁡(1+ra).e^{r_c} = 1 + r_a \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad r_c = \ln(1 + r_a).erc​=1+ra​⟺rc​=ln(1+ra​).

So 5% continuous is the same as about 5.13% annual. When you compare rates, always check which convention they use.

The same idea converts a rate compounded mmm times a year into its effective annual rate, the annually compounded rate that gives the same growth: reff=(1+r/m)m−1r_{\text{eff}} = (1 + r/m)^m - 1reff​=(1+r/m)m−1.

Worked example

From a monthly quoted rate to a five-year discount factor

  1. A savings account quotes 6% a year, compounded monthly. One year of growth is twelve months at 0.5% each; subtract 1 to get the effective annual rate.

    reff=(1+0.0612)12−1\displaystyle r_{\text{eff}} = \left(1 + \tfrac{0.06}{12}\right)^{12} - 1reff​=(1+120.06​)12−1=6.17%

  2. Discounting over 5 years means dividing by five years of that growth, that is 60 monthly periods.

    (1+0.0612)−60=(1+reff)−5\displaystyle \left(1 + \tfrac{0.06}{12}\right)^{-60} = (1 + r_{\text{eff}})^{-5}(1+120.06​)−60=(1+reff​)−5=0.7414

Summary

  • Money today is worth more than the same amount later, because it can earn the risk-free rate rrr.
  • Compounding moves money forward: BT=B0(1+r)TB_T = B_0(1+r)^TBT​=B0​(1+r)T, or B0erTB_0e^{rT}B0​erT in continuous time.
  • Discounting moves money back: PV=Ce−rT\text{PV} = Ce^{-rT}PV=Ce−rT. The factor e−rTe^{-rT}e−rT is the discount factor.

Every later lesson uses the discount factor e−rTe^{-rT}e−rT. Whenever a formula compares a future payment with a price today, this factor is how the two are put on the same footing.
