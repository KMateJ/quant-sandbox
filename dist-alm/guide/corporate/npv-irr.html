Net present value and internal rate of return

Lesson 3 of 10 · 1 min read

A firm constantly decides which projects to take on: a new machine, a new product line, a new plant. Each project costs money now and is expected to return money later. This lesson turns that comparison into two numbers, the net present value and the internal rate of return, using the discounting from "Interest, compounding and discounting".

Cash flows of a project

Describe a project by its cash flows: the amounts of money it pays out or brings in, by date. Write CFtCF_tCFt​ for the net cash flow at the end of year ttt, with t=0t = 0t=0 meaning today. A typical project has a negative CF0CF_0CF0​ (the investment) followed by positive cash flows CF1,…,CFNCF_1, \dots, CF_NCF1​,…,CFN​.

Two modelling choices are made here. Cash flows arrive at the end of each year, and the rate used to discount them is quoted with annual compounding. Both match the lab.

The discount rate

In this lesson rrr is the discount rate of the project: the annual return investors could earn elsewhere on an investment of the same risk. It is also called the opportunity cost of capital or the required return. It is not a risk-free rate; it is higher, because project cash flows are uncertain.

Net present value

Discount every cash flow back to today with the annual discount factor 1/(1+r)t1/(1+r)^t1/(1+r)t and add them up. The result is the net present value:

NPV(r)=CF0+CF11+r+CF2(1+r)2+⋯+CFN(1+r)N=∑t=0NCFt(1+r)t.\text{NPV}(r) = CF_0 + \frac{CF_1}{1+r} + \frac{CF_2}{(1+r)^2} + \cdots + \frac{CF_N}{(1+r)^N} = \sum_{t=0}^{N} \frac{CF_t}{(1+r)^t}.NPV(r)=CF0​+1+rCF1​​+(1+r)2CF2​​+⋯+(1+r)NCFN​​=t=0∑N​(1+r)tCFt​​.

The NPV is the value the project adds today. Investors could reproduce the project's future cash flows in the market for their present value; the project delivers them for the cost −CF0-CF_0−CF0​ instead. The difference is a gain (or loss) to the owners.

NPV rule: accept a project when NPV>0\text{NPV} > 0NPV>0. Among mutually exclusive projects, choose the one with the highest NPV.

A project with equal annual cash flows

Consider a project that costs 100 today and pays the same amount CCC at the end of each of the next NNN years. The cash flows after t=0t = 0t=0 form an annuity, and their present value has a closed form. Summing the geometric series gives

∑t=1NC(1+r)t=C⋅1−(1+r)−Nr,NPV(r)=−100+C⋅1−(1+r)−Nr.\sum_{t=1}^{N} \frac{C}{(1+r)^t} = C \cdot \frac{1 - (1+r)^{-N}}{r}, \qquad \text{NPV}(r) = -100 + C \cdot \frac{1 - (1+r)^{-N}}{r}.t=1∑N​(1+r)tC​=C⋅r1−(1+r)−N​,NPV(r)=−100+C⋅r1−(1+r)−N​.

Example: C=30C = 30C=30, N=5N = 5N=5 and r=10%r = 10\%r=10%.

The NPV profile

Plot the NPV against the discount rate. This curve is the NPV profile. At r=0r = 0r=0 it equals the plain sum of the cash flows (−100+5×30=50-100 + 5 \times 30 = 50−100+5×30=50 in the example). As rrr rises, later cash flows are discounted more heavily and the NPV falls. For the example project it is 29.8829.8829.88 at 5%5\%5%, 13.7213.7213.72 at 10%10\%10%, 0.560.560.56 at 15%15\%15% and −10.28-10.28−10.28 at 20%20\%20%.

Internal rate of return

The internal rate of return (IRR) is the discount rate at which the NPV is exactly zero:

∑t=0NCFt(1+IRR)t=0.\sum_{t=0}^{N} \frac{CF_t}{(1+\text{IRR})^t} = 0.t=0∑N​(1+IRR)tCFt​​=0.

It is where the NPV profile crosses the horizontal axis. There is no general formula for it; it is found numerically, by trying rates until the NPV changes sign and then narrowing the interval. For the example project the IRR is about 15.24%15.24\%15.24%. Read it as the annual return the project itself earns on the money tied up in it.

For a project with one outflow followed by inflows, the profile falls steadily, so the IRR rule agrees with the NPV rule: the NPV is positive exactly when r<IRRr < \text{IRR}r<IRR.

Raise CCC, lengthen NNN or lower rrr until the NPV crosses zero. Moving rrr slides along the curve without changing it, so the IRR stays fixed.

Profitability index

The profitability index divides the present value of the inflows by the initial investment:

PI=∑t=1NCFt/(1+r)t−CF0.\text{PI} = \frac{\sum_{t=1}^{N} CF_t/(1+r)^t}{-CF_0}.PI=−CF0​∑t=1N​CFt​/(1+r)t​.

In the example PI=113.72/100≈1.14\text{PI} = 113.72/100 \approx 1.14PI=113.72/100≈1.14: each unit invested returns 1.14 units of present value. PI>1\text{PI} > 1PI>1 is the same condition as NPV>0\text{NPV} > 0NPV>0. The index is useful when capital is limited and projects must be ranked by value per unit invested.

Where the IRR misleads

The IRR is popular because a percentage is easy to communicate, but it can give wrong answers in two situations.

Mutually exclusive projects. Project A costs 100 and returns 120 after one year; project B costs 100 and returns 150 after three years. A has an IRR of 20%20\%20%, B only about 14.47%14.47\%14.47%. At r=10%r = 10\%r=10%, however, the NPV of A is 120/1.1−100≈9.09120/1.1 - 100 \approx 9.09120/1.1−100≈9.09, and that of B is 150/1.13−100≈12.70150/1.1^3 - 100 \approx 12.70150/1.13−100≈12.70. B adds more value. The profiles cross at about 11.80%11.80\%11.80%; below that rate the NPV ranking and the IRR ranking disagree, and the NPV ranking is the right one, because it measures value in money, not in percent.

Cash flows that change sign more than once. A project with cash flows −100-100−100, +230+230+230, −132-132−132 has NPV zero at both 10%10\%10% and 20%20\%20%. Its profile rises and then falls, so there are two IRRs and neither is a meaningful rate of return. The NPV at the actual discount rate still answers the question.

Summary

  • A project is a list of dated cash flows; discount each at the project's discount rate rrr and add them to get the NPV.
  • Accept projects with positive NPV; among exclusive alternatives, take the highest NPV.
  • The IRR is the rate at which the NPV is zero, where the NPV profile crosses the axis.
  • The profitability index is the present value of the inflows per unit invested; PI>1\text{PI} > 1PI>1 exactly when NPV>0\text{NPV} > 0NPV>0.
  • For ranking exclusive projects and for cash flows that change sign several times, trust the NPV over the IRR.
