Geometric Brownian motion

Lesson 10 of 17 · 1 min read

This lesson builds the standard continuous-time model of a stock price, geometric Brownian motion (GBM). It combines the Brownian motion WtW_tWt​ from "Random walks and Brownian motion" with the log returns and volatility of "Returns and volatility", and it is the continuous limit of the trees in "Multi-step trees and backward induction". The Black–Scholes formula in the next lesson is computed under this model.

Modelling returns instead of prices

The simplest idea would be to let the price itself follow a Brownian motion, St=S0+μt+σWtS_t = S_0 + \mu t + \sigma W_tSt​=S0​+μt+σWt​. This has two defects. First, WtW_tWt​ is normally distributed, so the price can become negative. Second, a move of 1 means very different things for a stock at 10 and a stock at 1000. What should be random and comparable across stocks is the return, not the price change.

So we model the return over a short interval dtdtdt. The assumptions are:

  • The expected return per unit time is a constant μ\muμ (the drift).
  • The volatility of returns is a constant σ\sigmaσ.
  • The random part of the return comes from a Brownian motion: returns over non-overlapping intervals are independent and normally distributed.
  • The stock pays no dividends and its price moves continuously, without jumps.

The model equation

These assumptions are written as

dStSt=μ dt+σ dWt⟺dSt=μSt dt+σSt dWt.\frac{dS_t}{S_t} = \mu\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad dS_t = \mu S_t\,dt + \sigma S_t\,dW_t.St​dSt​​=μdt+σdWt​⟺dSt​=μSt​dt+σSt​dWt​.

Read it as a recipe for one small step: over dtdtdt the return has a deterministic part μ dt\mu\,dtμdt and a random part σ dWt\sigma\,dW_tσdWt​. Since dWtdW_tdWt​ is normal with mean 0 and variance dtdtdt, the return over the step has mean μ dt\mu\,dtμdt and standard deviation σdt\sigma\sqrt{dt}σdt​, which is the square-root-of-time rule from "Returns and volatility". Because both terms are proportional to StS_tSt​, price moves scale with the price level.

The solution

The equation has an explicit solution:

ST=S0exp⁡ ⁣((μ−12σ2)T+σWT).S_T = S_0 \exp\!\left( \left(\mu - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right) T + \sigma W_T \right).ST​=S0​exp((μ−21​σ2)T+σWT​).

Taking logarithms, the log return over [0,T][0, T][0,T] is

ln⁡STS0=(μ−12σ2)T+σWT∼N ⁣((μ−12σ2)T,  σ2T).\ln\frac{S_T}{S_0} = \left(\mu - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right) T + \sigma W_T \sim N\!\left( \left(\mu - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right) T,\; \sigma^2 T \right).lnS0​ST​​=(μ−21​σ2)T+σWT​∼N((μ−21​σ2)T,σ2T).

So the log price is a Brownian motion with a constant drift. A random variable whose logarithm is normal is called log-normal. Since STS_TST​ is S0S_0S0​ times an exponential, it is always positive: GBM fixes the first defect of the naive model.

The volatility correction

The drift of the log price is μ−12σ2\mu - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2μ−21​σ2, not μ\muμ. The correction comes from a property of the normal distribution. If Z∼N(m,s2)Z \sim N(m, s^2)Z∼N(m,s2), then

E[eZ]=em+12s2.\mathbb{E}\left[e^{Z}\right] = e^{m + \frac{1}{2}s^2}.E[eZ]=em+21​s2.

The exponential is convex, so large positive values of ZZZ are amplified more than negative ones are damped, and the mean of eZe^ZeZ exceeds eme^mem. Apply this with m=(μ−12σ2)Tm = (\mu - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2)Tm=(μ−21​σ2)T and s2=σ2Ts^2 = \sigma^2 Ts2=σ2T:

EP[ST]=S0 e(μ−12σ2)T+12σ2T=S0 eμT.\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[S_T] = S_0\, e^{(\mu - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2)T + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 T} = S_0\, e^{\mu T}.EP[ST​]=S0​e(μ−21​σ2)T+21​σ2T=S0​eμT.

The −12σ2-\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2−21​σ2 is exactly what is needed so that the expected price grows at rate μ\muμ, as assumed. The formal derivation uses Itô's lemma, the chain rule for functions of Brownian motion, which adds the term −12σ2 dt-\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2\,dt−21​σ2dt when we pass from dS/SdS/SdS/S to dln⁡Sd\ln SdlnS.

An everyday version of the same effect is volatility drag. A price that rises 10% and then falls 10% ends at 1.1⋅0.9=0.991.1 \cdot 0.9 = 0.991.1⋅0.9=0.99 of where it started: the average return is zero, but the compound growth is negative. The more volatile the returns, the larger the gap between the average return and the growth rate actually achieved.

Mean and median

Because the exponential is increasing, the median of STS_TST​ is the exponential of the median of the log price, which equals its mean:

mean=S0 eμT,median=S0 e(μ−12σ2)T.\text{mean} = S_0\, e^{\mu T}, \qquad \text{median} = S_0\, e^{(\mu - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2) T}.mean=S0​eμT,median=S0​e(μ−21​σ2)T.

The mean lies above the median: the log-normal distribution is skewed to the right, with a long tail of large outcomes pulling the average up. A typical path ends near the median, below the mean.

Example: S0=100S_0 = 100S0​=100, μ=8%\mu = 8\%μ=8%, σ=20%\sigma = 20\%σ=20%, T=1T = 1T=1. The mean is 100 e0.08≈108.33100\,e^{0.08} \approx 108.33100e0.08≈108.33, the median 100 e0.06≈106.18100\,e^{0.06} \approx 106.18100e0.06≈106.18. The log return is N(0.06, 0.22)N(0.06,\, 0.2^2)N(0.06,0.22), so the probability that the stock ends below 100 is N(−0.06/0.2)=N(−0.3)≈38%N(-0.06/0.2) = N(-0.3) \approx 38\%N(−0.06/0.2)=N(−0.3)≈38%, even though the expected return is positive. A 95% range for STS_TST​ is 100 e0.06±1.96⋅0.2100\,e^{0.06 \pm 1.96 \cdot 0.2}100e0.06±1.96⋅0.2, about 71.7 to 157.1, which is not symmetric around the median.

Link to the binomial tree

In a CRR tree with u=eσΔtu = e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}u=eσΔt​ and d=1/ud = 1/ud=1/u, each step adds ±σΔt\pm\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}±σΔt​ to the log price. That is the scaled coin-flip walk of "Random walks and Brownian motion". As the number of steps grows, the log price converges to a Brownian motion with volatility σ\sigmaσ plus a drift, and the terminal stock price becomes log-normal. GBM is the model that the trees approximate, which is why tree prices converge as nnn grows.

The risk-neutral version

"Risk-neutral probabilities" showed that for pricing we use the measure Q\mathbb{Q}Q under which the stock earns the risk-free rate. In GBM this means replacing μ\muμ by rrr and WWW by a Brownian motion WQW^{\mathbb{Q}}WQ under Q\mathbb{Q}Q:

dSt=rSt dt+σSt dWtQ,ST=S0exp⁡ ⁣((r−12σ2)T+σWTQ).dS_t = r S_t\,dt + \sigma S_t\,dW_t^{\mathbb{Q}}, \qquad S_T = S_0 \exp\!\left( \left(r - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right) T + \sigma W_T^{\mathbb{Q}} \right).dSt​=rSt​dt+σSt​dWtQ​,ST​=S0​exp((r−21​σ2)T+σWTQ​).

The volatility σ\sigmaσ stays the same; only the drift changes. Then EQ[ST]=S0erT\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[S_T] = S_0 e^{rT}EQ[ST​]=S0​erT, and the discounted price e−rtSte^{-rt}S_te−rtSt​ is a martingale under Q\mathbb{Q}Q, exactly as in the one-step tree. The real drift μ\muμ disappears from option prices, just as the real probability ppp did.

Summary

  • GBM models returns: dSt=μSt dt+σSt dWtdS_t = \mu S_t\,dt + \sigma S_t\,dW_tdSt​=μSt​dt+σSt​dWt​, with constant drift μ\muμ and volatility σ\sigmaσ.
  • Its solution is ST=S0exp⁡((μ−12σ2)T+σWT)S_T = S_0\exp((\mu - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2)T + \sigma W_T)ST​=S0​exp((μ−21​σ2)T+σWT​): log-normal and always positive.
  • The −12σ2-\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2−21​σ2 term makes the mean S0eμTS_0e^{\mu T}S0​eμT; the median S0e(μ−σ2/2)TS_0e^{(\mu - \sigma^2/2)T}S0​e(μ−σ2/2)T is lower.
  • For pricing, replace μ\muμ by rrr; the volatility is unchanged.

Under Q\mathbb{Q}Q, ln⁡ST∼N(ln⁡S0+(r−12σ2)T,  σ2T)\ln S_T \sim N\big(\ln S_0 + (r - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2)T,\; \sigma^2 T\big)lnST​∼N(lnS0​+(r−21​σ2)T,σ2T). The next lesson integrates the call payoff against this distribution to obtain the Black–Scholes formula.
