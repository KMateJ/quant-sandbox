Delta and hedging

Lesson 9 of 17 · 1 min read

In "The one-step binomial model" the number of shares Δ\DeltaΔ in the replicating portfolio appeared as the solution of two equations. This lesson shows that Δ\DeltaΔ is also a sensitivity: how much the option value changes when the stock price moves. That reading turns replication into a practical tool, hedging, and leads to a second sensitivity, gamma, which measures how well a hedge holds up. It builds on "Multi-step trees and backward induction".

Delta in a tree

Take one node of a binomial tree. From the current stock price SSS the stock moves to Su=uSS_u = uSSu​=uS or Sd=dSS_d = dSSd​=dS, and the call is then worth CuC_uCu​ or CdC_dCd​. The replicating portfolio holds Δ\DeltaΔ shares, with

Δ=Cu−CdSu−Sd.\Delta = \frac{C_u - C_d}{S_u - S_d}.Δ=Su​−Sd​Cu​−Cd​​.

This is a ratio of changes: change in option value divided by change in stock price. In the worked example of the one-step lesson (S0=100S_0 = 100S0​=100, u=1.2u = 1.2u=1.2, d=0.9d = 0.9d=0.9, K=100K = 100K=100) the call pays 20 or 0 and the stock ends at 120 or 90, so Δ=20/30≈0.667\Delta = 20/30 \approx 0.667Δ=20/30≈0.667. Each unit of stock movement moves the option by about two thirds of a unit.

Delta as a derivative

Let the time step Δt\Delta tΔt shrink, as in the multi-step trees. The two prices SuS_uSu​ and SdS_dSd​ move closer to SSS, and the difference quotient above becomes a derivative. Write C(S,t)C(S, t)C(S,t) for the option value as a function of the current stock price and time. Then

Δ=∂C∂S.\Delta = \frac{\partial C}{\partial S}.Δ=∂S∂C​.

Geometrically, Δ\DeltaΔ is the slope of the curve S↦C(S,t)S \mapsto C(S,t)S↦C(S,t) at today's price. For a call this curve is increasing and convex: deep out of the money it is flat (the option is nearly worthless whatever the stock does), deep in the money it runs parallel to the line S−Ke−rTS - Ke^{-rT}S−Ke−rT. So the delta of a call always lies between 0 and 1:

0≤Δcall≤1.0 \le \Delta_{\text{call}} \le 1.0≤Δcall​≤1.

A put has delta between −1-1−1 and 000, because its value falls when the stock rises.

The hedged portfolio

Suppose you own one call and want to remove your exposure to small stock moves. Sell Δ\DeltaΔ shares short. The combined position is

Π=C−ΔS.\Pi = C - \Delta S.Π=C−ΔS.

Over a short interval the stock changes by dSdSdS. To first order, the call changes by Δ dS\Delta\,dSΔdS and the short stock position changes by −Δ dS-\Delta\,dS−ΔdS, so

dΠ≈Δ dS−Δ dS=0.d\Pi \approx \Delta\,dS - \Delta\,dS = 0.dΠ≈ΔdS−ΔdS=0.

A position with zero first-order sensitivity to the stock is called delta-neutral. This is the replicating portfolio of the tree lessons read the other way round: instead of building the option from stock and bond, you cancel the option's stock exposure with stock.

Delta Δ=∂C/∂S\Delta = \partial C/\partial SΔ=∂C/∂S is the number of shares that replicates the option locally. Holding C−ΔSC - \Delta SC−ΔS removes exposure to small stock moves.

Gamma and the hedge error

The cancellation above is only first-order. A Taylor expansion of CCC in SSS, ignoring the passage of time over the short interval, gives

C(S+dS)≈C(S)+Δ dS+12 Γ (dS)2,Γ=∂2C∂S2.C(S + dS) \approx C(S) + \Delta\,dS + \tfrac{1}{2}\,\Gamma\,(dS)^2, \qquad \Gamma = \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial S^2}.C(S+dS)≈C(S)+ΔdS+21​Γ(dS)2,Γ=∂S2∂2C​.

Gamma Γ\GammaΓ is the curvature of the value curve: how fast delta itself changes when SSS moves. Substituting into the hedged portfolio, the linear terms cancel and what remains is

dΠ≈12 Γ (dS)2.d\Pi \approx \tfrac{1}{2}\,\Gamma\,(dS)^2.dΠ≈21​Γ(dS)2.

For a long call Γ>0\Gamma > 0Γ>0, so the hedged position gains a little on any large move, up or down. The error grows with the square of the move: double the move and the error is four times larger. Gamma is largest for options near the money and close to maturity, where the value curve bends most sharply.

A worked example

Take S0=100S_0 = 100S0​=100, K=100K = 100K=100, r=5%r = 5\%r=5%, σ=20%\sigma = 20\%σ=20%, T=1T = 1T=1. The call is worth about 10.45, with Δ≈0.637\Delta \approx 0.637Δ≈0.637 and Γ≈0.0188\Gamma \approx 0.0188Γ≈0.0188. A dealer who sold 100 calls hedges by buying about 63.7 shares.

If the stock jumps to 110, the tangent predicts a call value of 10.45+0.637⋅10≈16.8210.45 + 0.637 \cdot 10 \approx 16.8210.45+0.637⋅10≈16.82, while the true value is about 17.66. The gap is about 0.84 per call. For a drop to 90 the gap is about 1.01. The gamma estimate gives 12⋅0.0188⋅102≈0.94\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 0.0188 \cdot 10^2 \approx 0.9421​⋅0.0188⋅102≈0.94, between the two. For a move of only 1 the error is about 0.009, a hundred times smaller, as the square law predicts.

Rebalancing

Delta changes whenever SSS or ttt changes, so a hedge set up today is only correct today. Keeping the position delta-neutral means rebalancing: recomputing Δ\DeltaΔ and adjusting the share holding at regular intervals. In the tree this happens at every node, which is exactly what backward induction prescribes.

In practice rebalancing is discrete, say once a day. Between rebalances the stock moves by a finite amount and the hedge error 12Γ(dS)2\tfrac{1}{2}\Gamma(dS)^221​Γ(dS)2 accumulates. Rebalancing more often makes each error smaller (it is proportional to the square of the stock move), but trading costs grow with the number of trades. Think of steering a car: small, frequent corrections keep you in the lane, but each one costs a little effort. Perfect replication, as assumed in option pricing, corresponds to continuous rebalancing with no trading costs.

Summary

  • In a tree, Δ=(Cu−Cd)/(Su−Sd)\Delta = (C_u - C_d)/(S_u - S_d)Δ=(Cu​−Cd​)/(Su​−Sd​) is both the replicating share holding and the sensitivity of the option to the stock.
  • In the continuous limit Δ=∂C/∂S\Delta = \partial C/\partial SΔ=∂C/∂S, the slope of the value curve; for a call 0≤Δ≤10 \le \Delta \le 10≤Δ≤1.
  • The portfolio C−ΔSC - \Delta SC−ΔS is delta-neutral; its remaining change is 12Γ(dS)2\tfrac{1}{2}\Gamma(dS)^221​Γ(dS)2, with gamma the curvature.
  • Hedges must be rebalanced; discrete rebalancing leaves an error of about 12Γ(dS)2\tfrac{1}{2}\Gamma(dS)^221​Γ(dS)2 per interval, which shrinks as the intervals get shorter.
