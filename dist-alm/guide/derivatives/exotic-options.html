Exotic options: barriers, Asians and digitals

Lesson 17 of 17 · 1 min read

So far every option in this track paid a function of the final price STS_TST​ alone. This lesson looks at options whose payoff is either non-standard or depends on the whole price path: digital, barrier and Asian options. It uses the pricing rule from "Risk-neutral probabilities", the formula from "The Black–Scholes formula" and the simulation method from "Monte Carlo option pricing", and it shows where the static replication of "Static replication: Breeden–Litzenberger and Carr–Madan" stops working.

Exotic options

An option is called exotic when it is not a plain European or American call or put. There are two ways to depart from the standard contract. The payoff can be a different function of STS_TST​, such as a fixed cash amount. Or the payoff can be path-dependent: it depends on prices at dates before TTT, for example on the maximum or the average of the path.

The difference matters for hedging. A European payoff f(ST)f(S_T)f(ST​) can be built statically from bonds, a forward and vanilla options, as the Carr–Madan result showed. A path-dependent payoff cannot in general: two paths that end at the same STS_TST​ can pay different amounts, so no portfolio of instruments that pay a function of STS_TST​ can match it. The pricing rule is unchanged, though. The price is still the discounted Q\mathbb{Q}Q-expectation of the payoff XXX:

V0=e−rT EQ[X].V_0 = e^{-rT}\,\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[X].V0​=e−rTEQ[X].

Digital options

A digital (cash-or-nothing) call pays 1 at TTT if ST>KS_T > KST​>K and nothing otherwise. Its payoff is the indicator 1{ST>K}\mathbf{1}\{S_T > K\}1{ST​>K}, a step instead of a ramp. The expected payoff is the risk-neutral probability of finishing above the strike, which "The Black–Scholes formula" identified as N(d2)N(d_2)N(d2​). Discounting gives

Barrier options

A barrier option is a vanilla option that is switched off or on when the price touches a level BBB. A knock-out option dies, paying nothing, if the barrier is touched before TTT; a knock-in option comes to life only if it is touched. The most common example is the up-and-out call with B>KB > KB>K. Writing MTM_TMT​ for the maximum of the path up to TTT, its payoff is

X=(ST−K)+ 1{MT<B}.X = (S_T - K)^+\,\mathbf{1}\{M_T < B\}.X=(ST​−K)+1{MT​<B}.

The payoff is never larger than the vanilla payoff, so the up-and-out call is cheaper than the vanilla. It loses exactly the paths that matter most to a call holder: those that rise far. As BBB falls toward KKK its value goes to zero, because a path that finishes above K≥BK \ge BK≥B must have touched BBB. As BBB rises the barrier is touched less often and the price approaches the vanilla price.

Volatility acts in two directions. Higher σ\sigmaσ makes large final payoffs more likely, which raises the vanilla price, but it also makes touching BBB more likely. With a barrier close to the spot the second effect wins, and the up-and-out call can get cheaper as σ\sigmaσ rises.

In–out parity

Hold an up-and-in call and an up-and-out call with the same KKK, BBB and TTT. On every path exactly one of them is alive, and the live one pays (ST−K)+(S_T - K)^+(ST​−K)+. Together they pay the vanilla payoff on every path, so by the law of one price

C0in+C0out=C0.C_0^{\text{in}} + C_0^{\text{out}} = C_0.C0in​+C0out​=C0​.

Pricing one of the two barrier options therefore prices the other.

Discrete and continuous monitoring

A contract must say how the barrier is checked. Textbook formulas assume continuous monitoring: the price is watched at every instant. Real contracts often use discrete monitoring, for example at each daily close. A path can cross BBB between two observation dates and come back unnoticed, so a discretely monitored knock-out is knocked out less often and is worth more than its continuously monitored version. Asian options

An Asian option pays on the average price over a set of monitoring dates t1,…,tnt_1, \dots, t_nt1​,…,tn​ instead of the final price. The arithmetic-average Asian call pays

X=(AT−K)+,AT=1n∑k=1nStk.X = (A_T - K)^+, \qquad A_T = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n} S_{t_k}.X=(AT​−K)+,AT​=n1​k=1∑n​Stk​​.

An average moves less than the price itself: early prices in the average have had little time to drift away, and their errors partly cancel. For averaging over the whole period the volatility of the average is roughly σ/3\sigma/\sqrt{3}σ/3​, about 11.5% when σ=20%\sigma = 20\%σ=20%. Lower volatility means a smaller time value, so the Asian call is cheaper than the vanilla. Averaging also makes the contract hard to manipulate by moving the price on a single day.

A sum of log-normal prices is not log-normal, so the arithmetic Asian call has no closed form. The geometric average is log-normal and has a Black–Scholes-type formula; since it moves closely with the arithmetic one, it is a natural control variate in the sense of "Monte Carlo option pricing".

Pricing by Monte Carlo

For path-dependent payoffs a single draw of STS_TST​ is not enough: we need the whole path at the monitoring dates. Under Q\mathbb{Q}Q the stock follows geometric Brownian motion with drift rrr, and the exact solution steps from one date to the next with Δt=T/n\Delta t = T/nΔt=T/n:

Stk+1=Stkexp⁡((r−12σ2)Δt+σΔt Zk+1).S_{t_{k+1}} = S_{t_k}\exp\big((r - \tfrac12\sigma^2)\Delta t + \sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z_{k+1}\big).Stk+1​​=Stk​​exp((r−21​σ2)Δt+σΔt​Zk+1​).
  1. Simulate NNN paths at the nnn monitoring dates with independent standard normal ZZZ's.
  2. On each path record what the payoff needs: STS_TST​, the running maximum MTM_TMT​, the average ATA_TAT​.
  3. Compute each path's payoff, average over the NNN paths and multiply by e−rTe^{-rT}e−rT.

The same paths price all the contracts at once; only the payoff function changes. The standard error behaves as described in "Monte Carlo option pricing".

Path-dependent options rarely have closed forms, but they are priced by the same rule as any other claim: simulate paths under Q\mathbb{Q}Q, evaluate the payoff on each path, average and discount.

Summary

  • An exotic option has a non-standard payoff or one that depends on the path; path-dependent payoffs cannot in general be replicated statically with vanillas.
  • A digital call pays 1 if ST>KS_T > KST​>K; its price is e−rTN(d2)e^{-rT}N(d_2)e−rTN(d2​) and its delta spikes near expiry at the money.
  • An up-and-out call is cheaper than the vanilla, falls to zero as BBB approaches KKK, and satisfies C0in+C0out=C0C_0^{\text{in}} + C_0^{\text{out}} = C_0C0in​+C0out​=C0​; discrete monitoring knocks out less often.
  • Averaging lowers volatility, so an arithmetic Asian call is cheaper than the vanilla and has no closed form.
  • Monte Carlo prices all of them from the same simulated paths at the monitoring dates.
