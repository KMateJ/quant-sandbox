Stochastic volatility and the Heston model

Lesson 15 of 17 · 1 min read

"Implied volatility and the volatility smile" described a market fact that "The Black–Scholes formula" cannot explain: when each quoted option price is inverted for its own σ\sigmaσ, the implied volatilities form a smile or skew across strikes instead of a flat line. A model that produces this shape has to let volatility change. This lesson builds the Heston model, in which the variance itself moves randomly, and shows how two of its parameters shape the smile.

Constant volatility and its failure

In geometric Brownian motion the size of the random shocks never changes. Observed returns behave differently. Calm periods and turbulent periods alternate, so volatility clusters in time. Large falls in the price tend to come with rising volatility. And the distribution of returns has fatter tails than the normal distribution, especially on the downside.

A constant σ\sigmaσ cannot produce any of this. The natural fix is to make the variance a random process of its own, driven by a second source of randomness.

The Heston model

Write vtv_tvt​ for the instantaneous variance of the stock's returns, so the instantaneous volatility is vt\sqrt{v_t}vt​​. Under the risk-neutral measure Q\mathbb{Q}Q, with no dividends, the Heston model is

dSt=rSt dt+vt St dWt(1),dS_t = rS_t\,dt + \sqrt{v_t}\,S_t\,dW^{(1)}_t,dSt​=rSt​dt+vt​​St​dWt(1)​,
dvt=κ(θ−vt) dt+ξvt dWt(2),dv_t = \kappa(\theta - v_t)\,dt + \xi\sqrt{v_t}\,dW^{(2)}_t,dvt​=κ(θ−vt​)dt+ξvt​​dWt(2)​,

where W(1)W^{(1)}W(1) and W(2)W^{(2)}W(2) are Brownian motions with correlation ρ\rhoρ, that is dWt(1) dWt(2)=ρ dtdW^{(1)}_t\,dW^{(2)}_t = \rho\,dtdWt(1)​dWt(2)​=ρdt. The first equation is geometric Brownian motion from "Geometric Brownian motion", with σ\sigmaσ replaced by the moving quantity vt\sqrt{v_t}vt​​. The second equation describes how the variance moves.

The parameters

  • v0v_0v0​: the variance today. v0\sqrt{v_0}v0​​ is the current volatility.
  • θ\thetaθ: the long-run variance. Over long horizons vtv_tvt​ is pulled towards θ\thetaθ, so θ\sqrt{\theta}θ​ is the long-run volatility.
  • κ\kappaκ: the speed of mean reversion. The drift κ(θ−vt)\kappa(\theta - v_t)κ(θ−vt​) is positive when the variance is below θ\thetaθ and negative when it is above, and it is stronger the larger κ\kappaκ is.
  • ξ\xiξ: the volatility of variance (vol-of-vol). It sets how strongly the variance itself fluctuates.
  • ρ\rhoρ: the correlation between the shocks to the price and the shocks to the variance.

Without the random term the variance would relax exponentially towards θ\thetaθ. The random term does not change the average path, so the expected variance at time ttt is

EQ[vt]=θ+(v0−θ) e−κt.\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[v_t] = \theta + (v_0 - \theta)\,e^{-\kappa t}.EQ[vt​]=θ+(v0​−θ)e−κt.

The gap to θ\thetaθ halves every ln⁡2/κ\ln 2/\kappaln2/κ years. This is the half-life of a variance shock.

The factor vt\sqrt{v_t}vt​​ in front of dW(2)dW^{(2)}dW(2) makes the shocks shrink as the variance approaches zero. If mean reversion is strong enough compared with the vol-of-vol, the variance never reaches zero. This is the Feller condition:

2κθ≥ξ2.2\kappa\theta \ge \xi^2.2κθ≥ξ2.

When it fails the variance can touch zero and bounce back. The model still works, but simulations need care near zero.

After half a year the expected variance is 0.04+0.05 e−1≈0.05840.04 + 0.05\,e^{-1} \approx 0.05840.04+0.05e−1≈0.0584, a volatility of about 24.2%24.2\%24.2%. After one year it is 0.04+0.05 e−2≈0.04680.04 + 0.05\,e^{-2} \approx 0.04680.04+0.05e−2≈0.0468, about 21.6%21.6\%21.6%.

Correlation and the skew

For equities ρ\rhoρ is typically negative, often between −0.5-0.5−0.5 and −0.9-0.9−0.9. A negative ρ\rhoρ means that a fall in the price tends to come with a rise in variance. This is the leverage effect: as the share price drops, the firm's debt is larger relative to its equity, and the equity becomes riskier.

Consider what this does to the distribution of STS_TST​. Paths that fall gain variance and spread out further downwards. Paths that rise lose variance and stay closer together. The left tail becomes longer than the right. Out-of-the-money puts, which pay off in that left tail, become more valuable than under Black–Scholes, so their implied volatility is higher. Implied volatility then falls as the strike rises: the skew.

Vol-of-vol and the curvature

The parameter ξ\xiξ controls how much the variance moves. With ξ=0\xi = 0ξ=0 the variance follows its deterministic path and the model is close to Black–Scholes with a time-varying volatility, so the smile is nearly flat. A larger ξ\xiξ mixes calm paths with very turbulent ones. The resulting distribution of returns has a sharper peak and fatter tails on both sides than a normal distribution with the same variance. Options far from the money on either side gain value, and the smile curves upwards at both ends.

In the Heston model, ρ\rhoρ tilts the implied-volatility curve (negative ρ\rhoρ gives the equity skew), and ξ\xiξ bends it (larger vol-of-vol gives more curvature).

Pricing in the Heston model

The price of a European call is still the discounted risk-neutral expectation C0=e−rT EQ[(ST−K)+]C_0 = e^{-rT}\,\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[(S_T - K)^+]C0​=e−rTEQ[(ST​−K)+]. There is no formula as short as Black–Scholes, but there are two standard routes.

The first is simulation, as in "Monte Carlo option pricing". Split [0,T][0, T][0,T] into small steps Δt\Delta tΔt. In each step draw two independent standard normals Z1,Z2Z_1, Z_2Z1​,Z2​, set ε1=Z1\varepsilon_1 = Z_1ε1​=Z1​ and ε2=ρZ1+1−ρ2 Z2\varepsilon_2 = \rho Z_1 + \sqrt{1-\rho^2}\,Z_2ε2​=ρZ1​+1−ρ2​Z2​, so that the two shocks have correlation ρ\rhoρ, and update

vt+Δt=vt+κ(θ−vt) Δt+ξvt+ Δt ε2,v_{t+\Delta t} = v_t + \kappa(\theta - v_t)\,\Delta t + \xi\sqrt{v_t^+}\,\sqrt{\Delta t}\,\varepsilon_2,vt+Δt​=vt​+κ(θ−vt​)Δt+ξvt+​​Δt​ε2​,
St+Δt=Stexp⁡((r−12vt+)Δt+vt+ Δt ε1).S_{t+\Delta t} = S_t\exp\Big(\big(r - \tfrac{1}{2}v_t^+\big)\Delta t + \sqrt{v_t^+}\,\sqrt{\Delta t}\,\varepsilon_1\Big).St+Δt​=St​exp((r−21​vt+​)Δt+vt+​​Δt​ε1​).

Here vt+=max⁡(vt,0)v_t^+ = \max(v_t, 0)vt+​=max(vt​,0) guards against the small negative values that a discrete step can produce. Averaging the discounted payoff over many paths gives the price, with the standard error from "Monte Carlo option pricing".

The second route is semi-analytic. Heston showed that the characteristic function of ln⁡ST\ln S_TlnST​ has a closed form. The call price can then be written as S0P1−Ke−rTP2S_0P_1 - Ke^{-rT}P_2S0​P1​−Ke−rTP2​, the same shape as Black–Scholes, where P1P_1P1​ and P2P_2P2​ are probabilities computed by a one-dimensional numerical integral. This is fast enough to fit the five parameters to a whole surface of market prices.

Summary

  • The Heston model lets the variance vtv_tvt​ follow its own mean-reverting random process, correlated with the price.
  • v0v_0v0​ is today's variance, θ\thetaθ the long-run level, κ\kappaκ the reversion speed (half-life ln⁡2/κ\ln 2/\kappaln2/κ), ξ\xiξ the vol-of-vol and ρ\rhoρ the correlation.
  • The Feller condition 2κθ≥ξ22\kappa\theta \ge \xi^22κθ≥ξ2 keeps the variance away from zero.
  • Negative ρ\rhoρ produces the equity skew; larger ξ\xiξ produces a more curved smile.
  • Prices come from simulation or from a numerical integral of the characteristic function.
