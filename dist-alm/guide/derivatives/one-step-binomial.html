The one-step binomial model

Lesson 5 of 17 · 1 min read

This lesson builds the simplest model in which an option has a definite price. It uses the payoffs from "Forwards, calls and puts" and the replication argument from "Arbitrage and the forward price": if a portfolio of traded assets pays exactly what the option pays, the option must cost what the portfolio costs. Here we construct that portfolio explicitly.

The model

There is one period, from today (time 0) to a single future date (time 1), and two traded assets:

  • A stock with price S0S_0S0​ today. At time 1 it is worth either uS0uS_0uS0​ (the up state) or dS0dS_0dS0​ (the down state), with d<ud < ud<u. The numbers uuu and ddd are the up factor and down factor.
  • A risk-free bond (or bank account). One unit invested today is worth 1+r1+r1+r at time 1 in both states. In the one-step lessons rrr is the simple rate for the whole period, so money grows by the factor (1+r)(1+r)(1+r), not erTe^{rT}erT.

We also assume that both assets can be bought or sold short in any quantity, including fractions, with no transaction costs and no dividends, and that borrowing and lending happen at the same rate rrr.

The model says nothing about how likely the up state is. A real-world probability ppp exists, but, as we will see, the option price does not use it.

The no-arbitrage condition

The model is only sensible if neither asset dominates the other. If 1+r≥u1+r \ge u1+r≥u, the bond pays at least as much as the stock in every state, so shorting the stock and buying the bond is an arbitrage. If 1+r≤d1+r \le d1+r≤d, the stock beats the bond in every state, and borrowing to buy the stock is an arbitrage. So we require

d<1+r<u.d < 1 + r < u.d<1+r<u.

In words: the risk-free growth factor must lie strictly between the two possible stock growth factors.

Price equals the cost of replication

The portfolio and the call have the same value in both states at time 1. If they had different prices today, you could buy the cheaper one, sell the dearer one, and keep the difference with no risk at time 1. That is an arbitrage, so the law of one price forces

C0=ΔS0+B.C_0 = \Delta S_0 + B.C0​=ΔS0​+B.

In the one-step binomial model the option price is the cost today of the stock-and-bond portfolio that reproduces its payoff in both states. No probabilities and no attitudes to risk enter this argument.

Summary

  • The one-step model has a stock moving to uS0uS_0uS0​ or dS0dS_0dS0​ and a bond growing by 1+r1+r1+r, with d<1+r<ud < 1+r < ud<1+r<u to rule out arbitrage.
  • Any payoff at time 1 is replicated by Δ=(Cu−Cd)/(uS0−dS0)\Delta = (C_u - C_d)/(uS_0 - dS_0)Δ=(Cu​−Cd​)/(uS0​−dS0​) shares and B=(Cd−ΔdS0)/(1+r)B = (C_d - \Delta dS_0)/(1+r)B=(Cd​−ΔdS0​)/(1+r) in the bond.
  • By the law of one price the option costs C0=ΔS0+BC_0 = \Delta S_0 + BC0​=ΔS0​+B.
  • The real-world probability of the up move never appears in the price.

"Risk-neutral probabilities" rewrites ΔS0+B\Delta S_0 + BΔS0​+B as a discounted weighted average of CuC_uCu​ and CdC_dCd​, which is the form that carries over to every later pricing model.
