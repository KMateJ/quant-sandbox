The Black–Scholes formula

Lesson 11 of 17 · 1 min read

This lesson derives the Black–Scholes price of a European call and put. It puts together three earlier results: pricing as a discounted risk-neutral expectation ("Risk-neutral probabilities"), the log-normal stock price under Q\mathbb{Q}Q ("Geometric Brownian motion") and put–call parity ("Put–call parity"). It also connects to "Delta and hedging", since the formula comes with an explicit delta.

Assumptions

The Black–Scholes model makes the following assumptions. Each one is a simplification, and the last section returns to the ones that matter most.

  • The stock follows geometric Brownian motion with constant volatility σ\sigmaσ and pays no dividends.
  • The risk-free rate rrr is constant and continuously compounded; you can borrow and lend at rrr.
  • Trading is continuous, there are no transaction costs or taxes, and short selling is allowed.
  • There is no arbitrage.
  • The option is European: it can be exercised only at maturity TTT.

Under these assumptions the option can be replicated by continuous delta hedging, so its price is the cost of replication. As in the tree lessons, that cost equals the discounted expected payoff under the risk-neutral measure Q\mathbb{Q}Q.

The price as an expectation

For a call with strike KKK the payoff is X=(ST−K)+X = (S_T - K)^+X=(ST​−K)+, so

C0=e−rT EQ[(ST−K)+].C_0 = e^{-rT}\,\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[(S_T - K)^+\right].C0​=e−rTEQ[(ST​−K)+].

Under Q\mathbb{Q}Q the stock is GBM with drift rrr. Writing WTQ=T ZW_T^{\mathbb{Q}} = \sqrt{T}\,ZWTQ​=T​Z with Z∼N(0,1)Z \sim N(0,1)Z∼N(0,1),

ST=S0exp⁡ ⁣((r−12σ2)T+σT Z).S_T = S_0 \exp\!\left( \left(r - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)T + \sigma\sqrt{T}\,Z \right).ST​=S0​exp((r−21​σ2)T+σT​Z).

The Black–Scholes differential equation

This expectation can be reached another way, which is how Black and Scholes originally argued. Hold a portfolio that is long one option worth V(S,t)V(S,t)V(S,t) and short Δ\DeltaΔ shares. By Itô's lemma the option value changes by

dV=(∂V∂t+12σ2S2∂2V∂S2)dt+∂V∂S dS.dV = \left( \frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} \right) dt + \frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\,dS.dV=(∂t∂V​+21​σ2S2∂S2∂2V​)dt+∂S∂V​dS.

Choosing Δ=∂V/∂S\Delta = \partial V/\partial SΔ=∂V/∂S cancels the random dSdSdS term, so the hedged portfolio Π=V−ΔS\Pi = V - \Delta SΠ=V−ΔS is riskless and must earn the rate rrr. Setting its return equal to rΠr\PirΠ gives the Black–Scholes partial differential equation:

∂V∂t+12σ2S2∂2V∂S2+rS∂V∂S−rV=0.\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} + rS\frac{\partial V}{\partial S} - rV = 0.∂t∂V​+21​σ2S2∂S2∂2V​+rS∂S∂V​−rV=0.

Every European option on the stock satisfies the same equation; only the terminal condition differs. For the call it is V(S,T)=(ST−K)+V(S,T) = (S_T - K)^+V(S,T)=(ST​−K)+, and solving the PDE with that boundary condition gives exactly the formula derived below. The Feynman–Kac theorem makes the two routes equivalent: it rewrites the solution of this PDE as the discounted risk-neutral expectation.

Deriving the formula

The computation takes three steps.

  1. Find when the option is exercised. ST>KS_T > KST​>K is equivalent to Z>−d2Z > -d_2Z>−d2​, where d2=ln⁡(S0/K)+(r−12σ2)TσTd_2 = \dfrac{\ln(S_0/K) + (r - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}d2​=σT​ln(S0​/K)+(r−21​σ2)T​.
  2. Split the payoff: (ST−K)+=ST 1{ST>K}−K 1{ST>K}(S_T - K)^+ = S_T\,\mathbf{1}_{\{S_T > K\}} - K\,\mathbf{1}_{\{S_T > K\}}(ST​−K)+=ST​1{ST​>K}​−K1{ST​>K}​, where 1\mathbf{1}1 is 1 if the event happens and 0 otherwise.
  3. Take the expectation of each part separately and discount.

The second part is the easy one. Since ZZZ is standard normal and NNN is symmetric,

Q(ST>K)=Q(Z>−d2)=N(d2).\mathbb{Q}(S_T > K) = \mathbb{Q}(Z > -d_2) = N(d_2).Q(ST​>K)=Q(Z>−d2​)=N(d2​).

For the first part, write φ\varphiφ for the standard normal density. Completing the square in the exponent gives eσTz−12σ2T φ(z)=φ(z−σT)e^{\sigma\sqrt{T}z - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 T}\,\varphi(z) = \varphi(z - \sigma\sqrt{T})eσT​z−21​σ2Tφ(z)=φ(z−σT​), so

Define d1=d2+σTd_1 = d_2 + \sigma\sqrt{T}d1​=d2​+σT​. Discounting both parts by e−rTe^{-rT}e−rT gives the Black–Scholes formula:

C0=S0 N(d1)−Ke−rTN(d2),d1,2=ln⁡(S0/K)+(r±12σ2)TσT.C_0 = S_0\,N(d_1) - K e^{-rT} N(d_2), \qquad d_{1,2} = \frac{\ln(S_0/K) + \left(r \pm \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}.C0​=S0​N(d1​)−Ke−rTN(d2​),d1,2​=σT​ln(S0​/K)+(r±21​σ2)T​.

The real drift μ\muμ does not appear anywhere. Two investors who disagree about the expected return but agree on σ\sigmaσ agree on the price.

Reading the two terms

The formula has the shape "stock received minus strike paid, each weighted by something".

  • N(d2)N(d_2)N(d2​) is the Q\mathbb{Q}Q-probability that the call is exercised, so Ke−rTN(d2)Ke^{-rT}N(d_2)Ke−rTN(d2​) is the present value of the expected strike payment.
  • S0N(d1)S_0 N(d_1)S0​N(d1​) is the present value of receiving the stock in the states where the call is exercised. It is larger than S0N(d2)S_0 N(d_2)S0​N(d2​) because exercise happens exactly when STS_TST​ is high.
  • N(d1)=∂C0/∂S0N(d_1) = \partial C_0/\partial S_0N(d1​)=∂C0​/∂S0​ is the call's delta: the replicating portfolio holds N(d1)N(d_1)N(d1​) shares and borrows Ke−rTN(d2)Ke^{-rT}N(d_2)Ke−rTN(d2​).

The last point is the continuous version of the tree result C=ΔS+BC = \Delta S + BC=ΔS+B, with Δ=N(d1)\Delta = N(d_1)Δ=N(d1​) and B=−Ke−rTN(d2)B = -Ke^{-rT}N(d_2)B=−Ke−rTN(d2​). Differentiating the formula also produces terms from d1d_1d1​ and d2d_2d2​ depending on S0S_0S0​, but they cancel exactly.

C0=S0N(d1)−Ke−rTN(d2)C_0 = S_0N(d_1) - Ke^{-rT}N(d_2)C0​=S0​N(d1​)−Ke−rTN(d2​), with d2=d1−σTd_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T}d2​=d1​−σT​. N(d2)N(d_2)N(d2​) is the risk-neutral exercise probability and N(d1)N(d_1)N(d1​) is the delta.

How the price depends on the inputs

  • Stock price S0S_0S0​: the call rises and the put falls; the slopes are the deltas.
  • Volatility σ\sigmaσ: both prices rise. The holder gains from large moves on one side and loses at most the premium on the other.
  • Maturity TTT: without dividends the call rises with TTT; more time means more spread in STS_TST​ and a smaller present value of the strike.
  • Rate rrr: the call rises and the put falls, because the strike is paid later and discounted more.

Two limits check the formula. As σ→0\sigma \to 0σ→0 the stock grows deterministically at rrr and C0→(S0−Ke−rT)+C_0 \to (S_0 - Ke^{-rT})^+C0​→(S0​−Ke−rT)+, the lower bound from "Put–call parity". As T→0T \to 0T→0 the price tends to the payoff (S0−K)+(S_0 - K)^+(S0​−K)+.

Limitations of the model

Market option prices can be turned back into a volatility: the implied volatility is the σ\sigmaσ that makes the Black–Scholes formula match the observed price. If the model were exact, every option on the same stock would have the same implied volatility. In practice, implied volatility varies with strike (the volatility smile or skew, with low strikes usually more expensive) and with maturity.

The main causes are assumptions that fail: volatility is not constant but changes randomly and tends to rise when prices fall; prices sometimes jump; and returns have fatter tails than the normal distribution. Models such as Heston keep the risk-neutral pricing framework but let the volatility follow its own random process, which produces a smile. Black–Scholes remains the common language: prices are quoted as implied volatilities, and its delta is the starting point for hedging.

  • Under the Black–Scholes assumptions, C0=e−rTEQ[(ST−K)+]C_0 = e^{-rT}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[(S_T - K)^+]C0​=e−rTEQ[(ST​−K)+] with STS_TST​ log-normal and drift rrr.
  • The result is C0=S0N(d1)−Ke−rTN(d2)C_0 = S_0N(d_1) - Ke^{-rT}N(d_2)C0​=S0​N(d1​)−Ke−rTN(d2​); the put follows from parity.
  • N(d2)N(d_2)N(d2​) is the risk-neutral exercise probability, N(d1)N(d_1)N(d1​) the delta; the real drift μ\muμ does not enter.
  • Prices rise with σ\sigmaσ; the volatility smile shows that a single constant σ\sigmaσ does not fit the market, which the Heston lab addresses.
